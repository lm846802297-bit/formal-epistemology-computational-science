Machine-Checked Proofs and Formalization Interfaces in Mathematical Physics

## The Epistemology of Osterwalder–Schrader Reconstruction


# Abstract

Machine-checked proofs can establish mathematical consequences with high deductive reliability, but the scientific use of such results often involves more than one formal representation of a physical theory. This paper asks under what conditions a theorem proved in one formalization can warrant an inference in another. The question is examined through three public Lean developments associated with Osterwalder–Schrader reconstruction: OSforGFF, which constructs a massive Gaussian free field in a Glimm–Jaffe probability-measure formulation; OSreconstruction, which proves conditional distributional reconstruction theorems from zero-diagonal Euclidean Schwinger data; and a Comparator project that checks the reconstruction statements through an independently elaborated specification and explicit record-equivalence bridges. The analysis distinguishes local proof warrant, correspondence between a source formalism and its encoding, inter-formalization warrant, hypothesis instantiation, and target-directed scientific inference. The case shows both a positive instance of warrant transfer, where the Comparator bridges preserve the relevant Schwinger family and growth assumptions, and a missing public interface, since the OSforGFF construction is not formally instantiated as an input to the OSreconstruction package at the examined revisions. The paper uses inter-formalization warrant as an epistemological label for this interface requirement, not as a new proof-transfer protocol; scientific representation and empirical warrant remain further, distinct questions.

**Keywords:** machine-checked proof; mathematical physics; formalization; scientific representation; Osterwalder–Schrader reconstruction; epistemology.


# 1. Introduction

The increasing use of interactive theorem provers in mathematical physics raises a question about the relation between formal proof and scientific knowledge. A proof assistant such as Lean can verify that a proposition follows from specified definitions and assumptions in a formal system. When a proof is accepted by the kernel, the resulting warrant is deductive: relative to the soundness of the system and its declared trust base, the proposition follows from the assumptions as they are represented in the formal language. This is a substantial achievement, particularly in areas of mathematical physics where arguments depend on long chains of functional-analytic estimates, distributional constructions, and transformations between different mathematical descriptions. Yet the scientific use of a checked theorem may involve several further steps. The formal statement must correspond to the relevant mathematical content of the physical theory; a concrete model must satisfy the theorem's hypotheses; and, where the result is used to make a claim about a physical target, the model-to-target inference must be justified. These relations become especially important when a theory is formalized in more than one mathematical representation.

The present paper investigates this problem through the Osterwalder–Schrader (OS) reconstruction programme. Its central question is: under what conditions can a machine-checked theorem in one formalization support an inference in another formalization of the same physical theory? The question concerns neither the reliability of Lean's kernel in isolation nor the general relation between mathematical models and reality. It concerns the interfaces through which deductive warrant is transferred between formal representations of scientific mathematics. We use the term *inter-formalization warrant* for the warrant supplied by an adequately established correspondence between such representations. The term is intended as an analytical distinction, not as the name of a new general theory of scientific knowledge.

The problem is related to, but not exhausted by, several existing discussions. Work on formalizing chemical physics has shown how Lean can make assumptions in scientific derivations explicit and how common structures can facilitate the reuse of results across theories. Bobbin et al. (2024), for example, develop a network of definitions for thermodynamics and kinematics and use it to establish interoperable proofs of gas laws and equations of motion. They also distinguish mathematical verification from the empirical adequacy of the theories being formalized. Their work establishes the practical importance of reusable formal structures; the present paper asks a different question about the warrant carried by correspondences between independently developed formal records and theorem interfaces. At a more abstract level, the formal-logical literature already provides a precise account of proof-preserving translations. Rabe (2017) defines theory morphisms as compositional translations between formal theories and proves judgment preservation: a derivation in the source theory yields a translated derivation in the target theory. Such morphisms also induce a corresponding model-reduction construction. This establishes the formal principle that theorem transfer can be justified by a proof-preserving interpretation. The present paper does not propose a new theory morphism or a new proof-transfer mechanism; it asks what additional epistemic work is required when the theories being related are formal representations of a physical formalism and the resulting mathematical claims are used in scientific reasoning.

Their concern is adjacent to a technical literature on proof transfer and heterogeneous formal reasoning. Al Wardani et al. (2023) develop the Distributed Assertion Management Framework, in which formal assertions can be exchanged with explicit provenance and configurable trust in their creators and tools. Guilloud et al. (2025) develop SC-TPTP to transfer and reconstruct proof derivations between automated and interactive theorem provers, including Lean. These projects establish that proof exchange, translation, and trust management are already active technical research areas. The present paper does not claim to introduce proof transfer or interoperability. It examines a different epistemic question: when the formal records being connected encode distinct mathematical presentations of a physical theory, what must be established about their mathematical and physical content before a checked result can support scientific inference?

This issue is particularly visible in recent work on the formalization of quantum field theory. Douglas et al. (2026) explain that the same physical ideas can be expressed in mathematically different ways and that formalization requires concrete choices influenced by the definitions and theorems already available in Lean libraries. Their formalization of the massive free field follows the Glimm–Jaffe probability-measure formulation of the OS axioms, rather than the original Schwinger-function formulation. They explicitly note that this is a variant of the original OS axioms and explain the relation between their use of Schwartz functions and the compactly supported test functions in the Glimm–Jaffe presentation. Their paper identifies the OS reconstruction theorem as a subsequent milestone for that development. The OSforGFF repository has since been extended to a dimension-generic result, but its mathematical representation remains the probability-measure and generating-functional formulation described by the authors.

Related work on autoformalization has identified a further source of difficulty. Meadows et al. (2026) characterize forms of semantic drift that occur when informal scientific statements are translated into Lean, including notational collapse and abstraction elevation. Their focus is the human-in-the-loop autoformalization of physics problems, whereas the present study concerns established mathematical-physics developments and their relations to one another. Nevertheless, their analysis reinforces the point that kernel correctness is a property of the formal statement actually encoded; it does not alone establish that the statement preserves the intended scientific content. DeDeo and Duede (2026) formulate a corresponding problem for mathematical proof, distinguishing adequate representation of the original theorem from tracking the steps of the original argument. These criteria concern the relation between informal and formal proofs. We extend the analysis to a further level: the relation between distinct formal representations of mathematical structures and hypotheses within a scientific theory.

The philosophical background is provided by work on scientific representation and mathematical practice. Hughes's (1997) account of denotation, demonstration, and interpretation (DDI) distinguishes the representational relation between a model and its target from the reasoning performed within the model and the interpretation of the resulting conclusions (Hughes, 1997). Gelfert (2011, 2022), however, argues that the DDI and matching-model accounts each have shortcomings, and that scientific representation is often heterogeneous. Mature mathematical formalisms—physically interpreted systems of notation and locally applicable rules—can function as inferential and interpretative resources that are not reducible to a single representational relation. This point is important here: machine-checked proof should not be identified with scientific demonstration as a whole. It is one formalized component of reasoning conducted within a mathematical formalism. Gelfert's account of rigorous results and cross-model warrant transfer (2009) provides a further antecedent. It shows how mathematically established relations can transmit empirical warrant between physical models. The present paper does not claim that such transfer is new; it asks how formal proof and the interfaces between formalizations contribute to the deductive part of that process.

The distinction between local proof warrant and inter-formalization warrant is also consistent with the epistemological heterogeneity of computational science discussed by Imbert and Ardourel (2023). Verification can serve different epistemic roles depending on the object and purpose of the computational practice. A machine-checked proof may warrant a formal consequence, while correspondence with a source theory, instantiation in a model, and empirical assessment remain distinct tasks. The OS case makes these tasks unusually explicit because the projects expose their formal records, theorem hypotheses, test-function spaces, and proof-checking interfaces.

Three public Lean developments are examined. OSforGFF (Cherkis et al., 2026; commit fef84a82937dfd2bb7f672108a72da329d3ef0fd) constructs the massive Gaussian free field as a probability measure on tempered distributions and proves a dimension-generic Glimm–Jaffe package of OS properties. OSreconstruction (Yin et al., 2026; commit cf0904e34a7c146a9f312f601e3707dca4a87697) formalizes forward and reverse reconstruction results between Euclidean Schwinger data on a zero-diagonal Schwartz test-function space and Wightman-function distributions on the full Schwartz space. The OSreconstruction-comparator project (Douglas, Yin, et al., 2026; commit 93cdb40e728ba27b7b1b55efc30d57f7b43de9aa) supplies a Mathlib-only challenge and a solution that transfers the production theorems through explicit equivalences between independently elaborated records.

The case yields two contrasting findings. First, the Comparator project contains a positive example of inter-formalization warrant: the bridge theorems identify the independent and production OS records, preserve the Schwinger-function family, translate the growth hypotheses, and establish equivalence between the corresponding reconstruction propositions. This is not a second independent mathematical derivation; it is a formally checked correspondence through which the production proofs are made available under a separate specification. Second, the public artifacts do not expose an analogous end-to-end bridge from the particular OSforGFF probability-measure construction to the exact OS and growth-condition records required by OSreconstruction. The OSforGFF and OSreconstruction projects use different mathematical records and different test-function domains. In particular, the OS1 regularity condition in OSforGFF is not the arity-dependent OS-II growth condition in OSreconstruction. The latter package reports that examples of its corrected growth condition, including a massive free field, have been formalized in a private companion project; that report does not establish that the specific OSforGFF object at the pinned revision is the object instantiated in the private development.

The paper's contribution is a source-critical analysis of these interfaces and their epistemic significance. It distinguishes (i) the local deductive warrant supplied by kernel checking, (ii) correspondence between a source physical formalism and its formal encoding, (iii) inter-formalization warrant established by bridges between formal records, (iv) instantiation of theorem hypotheses by a concrete model, and (v) target-directed inference from a model to a physical system. These are not stages that every scientific claim must traverse in a single linear sequence. They are distinct relations that must be assessed whenever a claim relies on the composition of formalized results. The analysis does not propose a new general theory of scientific representation or empirical warrant transfer. Rather, it specifies an additional interface question that arises when machine-checked results are reused across independently formalized representations of a physical theory.

The method is a conceptual and source-critical case study of the theorem statements, definitions, bridge declarations, repository records, and original OS papers at fixed revisions. No new Lean implementation, numerical calculation, or independent proof replay is undertaken. The mathematical and formalization results are attributed to their authors, and repository-reported verification claims are distinguished from the source inspection performed here. Section 2 reconstructs the relevant mathematical interfaces in the OS projects. Section 3 develops the epistemological distinction between local proof warrant and inter-formalization warrant and relates it to scientific representation and model-based inference. Section 4 summarizes the implications for the epistemology of formalized mathematical physics.


# 2. The Osterwalder–Schrader Formalization Network

## 2.1. The mathematical setting

The Osterwalder–Schrader programme establishes conditions under which Euclidean field-theoretic data can be related to relativistic quantum field theory. Its Euclidean objects are Schwinger functions; the reconstructed relativistic objects are Wightman functions or distributions. The original OS papers formulate axioms for Euclidean Green functions and develop the corresponding reconstruction theorem (Osterwalder & Schrader, 1973, 1975). The 1975 paper explicitly extends and corrects the 1973 result by introducing the linear growth condition E0-prime. This correction is essential to the forward reconstruction theorem: the qualitative OS conditions E0–E4 alone do not supply the full hypothesis required by the corrected result.

The mathematical setting is not unique. Glimm and Jaffe (1987) formulate the Euclidean theory in terms of a probability measure on field configurations and its generating functional. The original OS formulation, by contrast, is expressed in terms of Schwinger functions as distributions on an appropriate test-function space. Douglas et al. (2026) choose the Glimm–Jaffe formulation for their Lean formalization because it makes the construction of a Euclidean field theory a problem about a functional measure, a mathematical structure well supported by Mathlib. They explicitly note that this is a variant of the original OS axioms and explain the relationship between their use of Schwartz functions and the compactly supported test functions in the Glimm–Jaffe presentation. The three formalization projects considered below should therefore be understood as related representations of the OS programme, not as interchangeable encodings of a single record.

## 2.2. OSforGFF: the probability-measure formulation

At commit fef84a82937dfd2bb7f672108a72da329d3ef0fd, OSforGFF (Cherkis et al., 2026) constructs the massive Gaussian free field as a probability measure on the space of tempered distributions S'(R^D), where D ≥ 2 is the spacetime dimension. The generic construction uses a covariance specified through a propagator typeclass, with explicit covariance instances in dimensions D=2,3,4,5. The principal theorem, gaussianFreeField_satisfies_all_OS_axioms_generic, proves that the constructed measure satisfies the project's bundled Glimm–Jaffe OS properties. The repository reports zero sorry declarations and no project-specific axioms for the pinned library theorem; these claims are understood relative to its Lean environment and foundational dependencies.

The axioms are predicates on the probability measure and its generating functional. OS0 states analyticity of the generating functional in finite collections of complex test-function parameters. OS1 is a regularity condition: there exist p in [1,2] and c>0 such that

\[
|Z_\mu(f)| \leq
\exp\left[
c\left(
\int_{\mathbb R^D}|f(x)|\,dx+
\int_{\mathbb R^D}|f(x)|^p\,dx
\right)
\right],
\]

together with a subsidiary local-integrability condition on the two-point function when p=2. For the massive Gaussian free field, the formal proof chooses p=2 and c=1/(2m^2), and proves the required two-point condition by identifying the mollified two-point function with the covariance kernel away from coincidence. OS2 expresses invariance under the Euclidean group. OS3 is reflection positivity in a complex generating-functional formulation, with time reflection and complex conjugation. OS4 is represented through clustering and ergodicity; the latter is formulated for a specified class of finite exponential functionals and established using a polynomial-clustering estimate.

These choices are not merely notational. The OSforGFF documentation and the accompanying paper explain how the formalized conditions relate to the Glimm–Jaffe presentation. In particular, the use of Schwartz test functions and tempered distributions is stated to imply the corresponding compactly supported test-function formulation, since D(R^D) is contained in S(R^D) and S'(R^D) is contained in D'(R^D). The authors also explain that their ergodicity condition is imposed on a dense class of finite exponential functionals and uses L2 convergence; they argue that density in L1 and the implication from L2 to L1 on a probability space recover the Glimm–Jaffe condition. These are substantive correspondence claims between the formal predicates and the source axioms. They are part of the mathematical interpretation of the formalization and should not be replaced by the bare observation that Lean accepts the proof.

The OS1 condition is particularly relevant to the comparison with OSreconstruction. It is the Glimm–Jaffe exponential regularity bound on the generating functional, with an additional two-point integrability condition. It is not the same predicate as the arity-dependent E0-prime growth condition required by the forward OSreconstruction theorem. The former controls the generating functional in terms of L1 and Lp norms of a single test function; the latter is a quantitative estimate on the family of n-point Schwinger distributions, with Schwartz seminorm orders increasing linearly with n. The shared language of regularity or growth does not establish a mathematical implication between them.

## 2.3. OSreconstruction: zero-diagonal Euclidean data and conditional reconstruction

At commit cf0904e34a7c146a9f312f601e3707dca4a87697, OSreconstruction (Yin et al., 2026) formalizes reconstruction for every positive spatial dimension d, with spacetime dimension d+1. Its Euclidean input is a family of Schwinger functions acting on the zero-diagonal Schwartz test-function space. Its Wightman distributions act on the full Schwartz space. The zero-diagonal domain is mathematically significant: it accommodates the coincidence singularities of Euclidean Schwinger functions without presupposing that they define distributions on the full Schwartz space.

The formal record OsterwalderSchraderAxioms includes temperedness, linearity and reality on the zero-diagonal test space; translation and connected Euclidean rotation invariance; reflection positivity on Borchers sequences supported in the ordered positive-time region; permutation symmetry; and the cluster property. The forward theorem os_to_wightman additionally assumes an OSLinearGrowthCondition. Schematically, the condition takes the form

\[
\|S_n(f)\|
\leq
\alpha\,\beta^n(n!)^\gamma
\|f\|_{ns,ns},
\]

where alpha and beta are positive, gamma is real, and the Schwartz seminorm orders grow linearly with the arity n. The exact Lean definition uses a supremum over the relevant weight and derivative indices and includes a normalization condition at arity zero. The theorem concludes the existence of a Wightman-function family whose distributions are Wick-paired with the input Schwinger family.

The package also formalizes the original-coordinate version of the OS-II growth condition. Its OSIIOriginalLinearGrowthCondition requires a positive Sobolev index s and the estimate

\[
\|S_n(f)\|
\leq
\alpha (n!)^\gamma
\|f\|_{\mathrm{OSII},ns},
\]

where the final seminorm is the original-coordinate seminorm on the zero-diagonal test space. The public arity-linear condition instead uses the maximum of the relevant Schwartz seminorms with weight and derivative orders bounded by ns, and allows the coefficient \(\alpha\beta^n(n!)^\gamma\). The theorem os_to_wightman_osii_original converts the original-coordinate record into the arity-linear record with the explicit parameter choice

\[
\alpha' = \max\{1,\alpha\},\qquad
\beta'=(d+2)^s,\qquad
\gamma'=\gamma+2s.
\]

It then concludes three results: existence of a Wick-paired Wightman family, the output growth estimate R0-prime, and uniqueness among Wightman families satisfying the same Wick-pairing relation. The conversion is a proved quantitative interface, not an identification by terminology. The package explicitly distinguishes this corrected arity-dependent condition from an earlier fixed-order growth predicate that it does not treat as an equivalent reconstruction input.

The reverse theorem constructs Euclidean Schwinger data from a Wightman-function family and proves the corresponding OS properties for that constructor. It does not assume the forward growth condition. The two directions therefore do not form an unconditional biconditional between arbitrary OS data and arbitrary Wightman data. The public endpoint is a family of Wightman distributions satisfying the formalized Wightman-function axioms, including covariance, spectral support, locality, positivity, Hermiticity, and clustering. The package explicitly places the subsequent GNS construction of a Hilbert space and operator-valued fields outside its scope.

The distributable OSreconstruction package does not itself expose inhabitants of its OS input record and corrected growth-condition record. Its README reports that a private companion project contains examples for the massive free field and the Proca Wick square. The report is attributed to the package authors; the private development is not independently inspectable here. Moreover, the existence of a massive free-field inhabitant in that companion project does not, by itself, identify it with the particular probability-measure object constructed by OSforGFF at the pinned revision. Establishing that identity or an appropriate correspondence is precisely an inter-formalization task.

## 2.4. Comparator: an explicit bridge between formal records

The OSreconstruction-comparator project (Douglas, Yin, et al., 2026), pinned at commit 93cdb40e728ba27b7b1b55efc30d57f7b43de9aa, provides a Mathlib-only challenge and a solution for the three reconstruction propositions. Its notion of independence is specific. The challenge does not import the production package; it reproduces verbatim the package's own Mathlib-only audit definitions from verification/comparator/Definitions.lean and appends the three theorem obligations. It is therefore an independently elaborated checking surface, not an independently authored mathematical specification or a second derivation by a separate research team.

The bridge files provide the important mathematical content. OSBridge.lean constructs conversions in both directions between the challenge's OS record and the production OsterwalderSchraderAxioms record. The Schwinger family is preserved definitionally. The bridge also identifies the challenge and production growth records, relates the Wick-pairing predicates, and establishes equality of the relevant output-growth predicates and original-coordinate seminorms. Solution.lean then proves equivalences between the challenge statements and the production specifications for the forward theorem, the OS-II theorem, and the reverse constructor. These equivalences preserve the underlying distribution families.

The Comparator checks that the challenge and solution share structurally corresponding declarations, that the proof dependencies remain within the stated whitelist of Lean's foundational axioms, and that the proofs can be replayed through a fresh kernel and NanoDa. The three equivalence results are identified as eToR_iff, eToROSII_iff, and rToE_iff. The project itself describes the repository as a thin wrapper containing no new mathematics. Its epistemic contribution is consequently not an independent mathematical discovery. It is a separately checked interface that makes the relation between a public theorem specification and the production formalization explicit and auditable. The challenge definitions are copied from the source project's own audit file, while the record adapters and theorem equivalences provide the bridge to the production records. The distinction between these roles is important: the challenge is independent at the level of imports and checking boundary, but not independent in authorship or mathematical origin.

The Comparator documentation also states several restrictions that delimit the propositions being checked. The forward tube includes an additional condition on the first imaginary coordinate; locality is formulated for adjacent transpositions rather than stated directly in its full permutation form; and reflection positivity is implemented through a totalized coercion that maps a test function failing the zero-diagonal condition to zero. The growth condition remains a forward hypothesis and is not recovered by the reverse construction. These are not erased by proof replay: the Comparator certifies the stated propositions with these conventions. Its formalization metadata labels the review status self-assessed and lists no external reviewers, while reporting review among the project authors. The checking boundary is therefore separate from the production imports, but it should not be described as an independent external audit.

## 2.5. What is and is not connected in the public formalization network

The three projects thus exhibit two different kinds of correspondence. Within the OSforGFF project, the authors explain how the probability-measure predicates relate to the Glimm–Jaffe axioms, including the use of Schwartz rather than compactly supported test functions and the restricted class used in the ergodicity statement. Within OSreconstruction and its Comparator, explicit Lean bridges identify independently elaborated records and preserve the Schwinger family, growth assumptions, and theorem conclusions. These are positive examples of correspondence work; they show that a formalization interface can itself become an object of mathematical proof.

The public artifacts do not, however, expose a single theorem that takes the specific OSforGFF construction and establishes that its associated Schwinger data inhabit the exact zero-diagonal OS record and arity-dependent growth condition required by OSreconstruction. Such a composition would require at least a map from the probability measure and generating functional to the relevant Schwinger distributions, a proof that the OS predicates transfer to the zero-diagonal formulation, and an inhabitant of the precise growth record. The different test-function domains and formal records cannot be identified merely because both projects concern the massive free field and the OS programme. The QFT formalization paper itself describes OS reconstruction as a subsequent milestone for the OSforGFF development. The private companion reported by OSreconstruction authors provides relevant additional evidence, but it does not make the specific public OSforGFF-to-OSreconstruction bridge inspectable.

The significance of the case does not depend on treating the missing public interface as a defect in either mathematical project. Each project proves the propositions it states. The issue is whether, and under what conditions, their results can be composed to support a further scientific claim. This is a question about the warrant of the interfaces between formalizations, not about the validity of the local proofs.


# 3. Inter-Formalization Warrant and Scientific Knowledge

## 3.1. Local proof warrant and the scope of machine checking

A machine-checked proof provides a strong form of deductive warrant for a proposition in a specified formal system. If a Lean kernel accepts a proof of

\[
\Gamma\vdash_{\mathcal L}\varphi,
\]

then, relative to the soundness of the formal system and its declared trust base, phi follows from Gamma as represented in L. This warrant is local: it concerns the formal proposition, the definitions through which its objects are represented, the assumptions on which it depends, and the imported declarations and kernel used in checking it. The OS case illustrates that locality does not make the warrant weak. OSforGFF proves a substantial collection of properties for a constructed Gaussian measure; OSreconstruction proves conditional reconstruction theorems; and the Comparator checks the correspondence between an independently elaborated audit surface and the production theorem statements. Each is a significant mathematical achievement.

The locality of proof warrant should not be confused with the claim that formalization is merely syntactic. As Douglas et al. (2026) emphasize, choosing definitions for a physical theory requires mathematical judgment. The available library, the structure of existing theorems, and the tractability of a proof can influence the representation adopted. The resulting formalization may be mathematically appropriate while differing from the most familiar formulation used by physicists. The epistemic task is therefore not to demand a word-for-word translation, but to establish that the formal representation preserves the mathematical content relevant to the claim being made.

This is where the correspondence problem discussed by DeDeo and Duede (2026) becomes relevant. Their distinction between adequate representation of an informal theorem and tracking the steps of an informal proof clarifies why the existence of a checked formal derivation is not, by itself, sufficient to establish correspondence with a source argument. In mathematical physics, the issue can arise before one asks whether a formal proof tracks an informal derivation: the formalized objects, test-function spaces, or predicates may represent a different version of the physical formalism. The OSforGFF choice of the Glimm–Jaffe probability-measure formulation is an explicit example. The formalization paper explains the relationship to the source axioms; that explanation is part of the warrant for interpreting the formal theorem as a result about the intended Euclidean field theory.

Gelfert's analysis of mature mathematical formalisms helps avoid an overly linear account of this relation. The DDI framework distinguishes denotation, demonstration, and interpretation, but Gelfert (2011) argues that successful scientific representation is often heterogeneous and depends on mature formalisms that supply both inferential and interpretative resources. Machine-checked proof should therefore not be identified with Demonstration as a whole. It is a formally articulated component of reasoning within a mathematical formalism. Nor should Denotation and Interpretation be treated as two final steps that occur only after a proof has been completed. The formalism itself can carry interpretative commitments and locally applicable rules that shape the meaning of the proof's objects and predicates.

## 3.2. From formalization correspondence to inter-formalization warrant

The OS case reveals a further problem when results from distinct formalizations are used together. A proof in one formalization does not automatically become a proof in another, even when both projects concern the same physical theory. The receiving formalization may use different records, test spaces, definitions of symmetry or positivity, or quantitative hypotheses. The warrant of the source theorem can be transferred only when the relevant correspondence between these structures is established.

We use inter-formalization warrant to refer to the warrant supplied by a verified interface through which a result in one formalization is made available in another. The notion is narrower than empirical warrant transfer between physical models and distinct from the general problem of translating an informal proof into a formal derivation. It concerns the relation between two formal representations. At a minimum, an interface must make explicit which objects correspond, how the relevant predicates and hypotheses are translated, and whether the conclusion and any witnesses are preserved. Where the theorem is conditional, the receiving formalization must also establish that its assumptions imply the translated source hypotheses. Where a reconstructed object is produced, the interface must identify the relevant output family rather than merely show that an object of a broadly similar type exists.

This can be expressed schematically. Suppose a source formalization establishes

\[
\Gamma_1\vdash_{\mathcal L_1}\varphi_1.
\]

A receiving formalization \(\mathcal L_2\) can use this result for a target proposition \(\varphi_2\) only if a translation or interpretation \(\tau\) preserves derivability and the interface establishes the required relations between assumptions and conclusions. One schematic audit condition is

\[
\begin{gathered}
\Gamma_1\vdash_{\mathcal L_1}\varphi_1,\qquad
\tau(\Gamma_1)\vdash_{\mathcal L_2}\tau(\varphi_1),\\
\Gamma_2\vdash_{\mathcal L_2}\tau(\Gamma_1),\qquad
\Gamma_2\vdash_{\mathcal L_2}\bigl(\tau(\varphi_1)\rightarrow\varphi_2\bigr)
\\
\Longrightarrow\quad
\Gamma_2\vdash_{\mathcal L_2}\varphi_2.
\end{gathered}
\]

The display is an audit schema, not a universal sufficient-condition theorem for scientific knowledge. The first line represents proof preservation under the translation; the second records hypothesis transport and conclusion preservation in the receiving formalization. The point is not that every interface must be an isomorphism or definitional equality. Depending on the application, it may be an equivalence of records, a structure-preserving map, or a proved implication. What matters is that the direction and strength of the correspondence are explicit and proved at the level required by the intended inference.

The Comparator bridge provides a concrete example. The challenge and production OS records are not simply assumed to be interchangeable. The bridge constructs conversions in both directions, proves that the Schwinger family is preserved, relates the growth-condition records, and identifies the Wick-pairing and output-growth predicates. The theorem equivalences then transport the forward, OS-II, and reverse reconstruction statements between the two specifications. This is an instance in which the interface itself carries a formal proof. The Comparator's fresh-kernel and NanoDa checks strengthen the auditability of that correspondence and of the transported proof. They do not constitute a second independent derivation of the OS reconstruction mathematics, because the challenge definitions reproduce the package's own audit definitions and the solution uses the production proofs through the bridge equivalences.

The proof-preservation component is familiar from the theory-morphism literature. Rabe (2017), for example, defines compositional translations between formal theories and establishes preservation of judgments and theorems under those translations. The schematic conditions above are therefore not offered as a new formal result. Their role here is diagnostic: they identify the proof-preserving component of an interface so that it can be distinguished from the additional question of whether the translated objects and predicates retain the intended physical interpretation and whether the receiving theorem's hypotheses are instantiated by the relevant model.

This use of the term should be distinguished from technical proof-system interoperability. Distributed assertion frameworks address the provenance and trust of assertions imported from other reasoning systems (Al Wardani et al., 2023), while SC-TPTP addresses the translation and reconstruction of proof derivations across automated and interactive provers (Guilloud et al., 2025). Such work concerns the preservation and checking of formal derivations across systems. The present analysis concerns the scientific-epistemic status of interfaces between mathematical records that may encode different formulations of a physical theory. The two problems can overlap, but they are not identical: proof transfer can succeed at the level of formal derivations while the correspondence between the encoded mathematical objects and the intended physical formalism remains to be assessed.

The distinction is also visible in the work of Bobbin et al. (2024). Their common structures for thermodynamics and kinematics are designed to facilitate reuse and interoperability among proofs. The present analysis does not claim that proof reuse or interoperable scientific structures are new. Rather, the OS case shows why the existence of an interface is an epistemic matter: when a result is reused across representations, the assumptions, predicates, and objects that make the transfer legitimate must be identified and preserved. The issue is especially consequential when the source and receiving formalizations are developed in different projects, use different test-function spaces, or formalize different versions of a familiar axiom system.

## 3.3. The OS case and the limits of composition

The contrast between the Comparator bridge and the OSforGFF-to-OSreconstruction interface illustrates two different epistemic situations. In the former, record equivalence and theorem transport are explicitly proved. In the latter, the public artifacts establish strong results in their respective formal settings, but do not expose a direct theorem connecting the specific OSforGFF measure to the exact OSreconstruction input record. This distinction is not resolved by noting that both developments concern the massive free field or invoke the OS axioms. The OSforGFF construction is formulated through a probability measure and generating functional on Schwartz functions; OSreconstruction takes a family of Schwinger distributions on a zero-diagonal Schwartz test space and requires an arity-dependent growth estimate. The mathematical passage between these records must be supplied.

The difference between the two growth conditions is particularly instructive. OSforGFF proves the Glimm–Jaffe OS1 exponential bound on the generating functional, with p=2 and c=1/(2m^2) for the massive Gaussian free field, together with the required local integrability of the two-point function. OSreconstruction requires a quantitative estimate on the family of Schwinger distributions whose Schwartz seminorm orders increase with the arity. These are different predicates on different mathematical objects. The first may be relevant to establishing regularity of the generating functional and the existence of moments; the second is an explicit hypothesis of the forward reconstruction theorem. A relation between them may be mathematically provable, but it cannot be inferred from their shared association with regularity or growth.

The OSreconstruction README reports that the corrected growth condition has concrete inhabitants in a private companion development, including the massive free field and the Proca Wick square. This report is relevant evidence that the hypothesis is not merely formal or vacuous. It does not, however, establish that the private inhabitant is identical to the OSforGFF object at the pinned revision, nor does it provide a publicly inspectable bridge from the OSforGFF generating-functional record to the OSreconstruction zero-diagonal Schwinger record. The distinction is between the existence of a suitable model in a companion development and the public formal transfer of the particular theorem result from one project to another.

This analysis also clarifies the relation to Gelfert's account of cross-model warrant transfer. Gelfert (2009) argues that mathematically rigorous relations can transmit empirical warrant between physical models, as in the relation between Hubbard and Heisenberg models. Inter-formalization warrant concerns a different, though related, level: it concerns the proof-preserving correspondence required before a mathematical result in one formal representation can be used in another. Such a correspondence may contribute to a rigorous relation between physical models, but it does not by itself establish the empirical warrant of either model or the physical relevance of the mapping. Machine checking can strengthen the deductive component of a warrant-transfer argument without generating empirical warrant independently.

## 3.4. From model-internal knowledge to target-directed inference

Once a theorem has been transported to the relevant formal representation and its hypotheses have been instantiated by a concrete model, the result supports a model-internal claim. This still does not automatically warrant a claim about an actual physical target. The DDI account and subsequent work on surrogate reasoning distinguish the validity of reasoning within a model from the soundness of the inference for the target (Suárez, 2004; Contessa, 2007). Frigg and Nguyen's (2018) DEKI account makes explicit the mappings through which properties exhibited by a model are keyed to and imputed to a target. Model adequacy is also purpose-sensitive: a model may be suitable for a mathematical or structural purpose without being adequate for quantitative prediction or empirical description (Parker, 2020).

The OS case should not be taken to imply that every machine-checked result must culminate in an empirical claim. Models may function as epistemic artifacts for investigating possible structures and modal relations, without representing a particular actual system (Knuuttila, 2021). The kind of warrant required depends on the claim being made. A formal theorem, a model-internal result, an explanatory claim, and a target-directed empirical inference are different epistemic achievements. Inter-formalization warrant concerns the transition between formal representations and should not be conflated with the model-to-target relation.

The resulting account is therefore not a linear pipeline in which kernel acceptance is followed automatically by physical interpretation and empirical confirmation. It is a set of distinguishable relations. Kernel checking warrants a derivation in a specified formal system. Formalization correspondence relates the encoded statement to its source mathematical formalism. Inter-formalization warrant relates distinct formal representations and permits theorem reuse when the relevant interfaces are proved. Hypothesis instantiation establishes that a concrete model satisfies the receiving theorem's assumptions. Target-directed inference requires an additional account of representation, purpose, and relevant evidence. These relations may be supported by different kinds of argument, and their requirements depend on the scientific claim at issue.

## 3.5. Implications for formalized mathematical physics

The OS case suggests that the epistemic significance of large formalization projects should not be assessed only by counting checked theorems or examining their local axiom footprints. Such information is important, but it does not describe how results can be reused across formal representations. A mature mathematical formalism often admits several mathematically distinct formulations, and a formalization project necessarily selects one of them. As the project develops, further formalizations may encode related objects and theorems in different records or libraries. The resulting network can support substantial reuse, but its epistemic reliability depends on the quality of the interfaces that connect its components.

This conclusion complements rather than replaces existing analyses. Bobbin et al. (2024) show how common mathematical structures can enable interoperable scientific proofs. Douglas et al. (2026) document how the choice of mathematical formulation is shaped by the available formal infrastructure and by the requirements of the physical theory. Meadows et al. (2026) identify semantic drift in the translation of scientific statements into formal language. DeDeo and Duede (2026) analyze the correspondence between informal proofs and formal derivations. The present case connects these issues at the level of independently formalized representations: it shows that the transfer of a checked result requires an explicit account of the interface, and that such an interface can itself be formalized and checked.

The contribution is deliberately limited. It is not a new general theory of scientific representation, a replacement for DDI, or a new account of cross-model empirical warrant. It is a formalization-aware distinction between local proof warrant and the warrant required to reuse a result across formal representations of a physical theory. The OS reconstruction network supplies a concrete setting in which this distinction can be examined: the Comparator demonstrates a proved interface, while the relation between OSforGFF and OSreconstruction remains a separate composition problem in the public artifacts inspected.


# 4. Conclusion

The Osterwalder–Schrader case shows that the epistemic significance of a machine-checked theorem depends not only on the validity of its proof within a formal system but also on the relations through which the result is connected to other representations of a physical theory. Kernel checking provides local deductive warrant for the formal proposition and assumptions that it verifies. When a result is reused across formalizations, an additional interface must establish the correspondence between the relevant objects, predicates, hypotheses, and conclusions. When the theorem is applied to a concrete model, its assumptions must be instantiated; when the result is used to make a claim about a physical target, the model-to-target inference requires further justification.

The three OS developments illustrate these distinctions. OSforGFF proves the Glimm–Jaffe probability-measure formulation of the OS properties for a dimension-generic massive Gaussian free field. OSreconstruction proves conditional distributional reconstruction theorems from zero-diagonal Schwinger data, with an arity-dependent growth condition in the forward direction. The Comparator project provides an explicit and checked bridge between its independently elaborated audit records and the production records, preserving the Schwinger family and the relevant growth and Wick-pairing predicates. The public artifacts do not, however, expose a corresponding end-to-end theorem that instantiates the exact OSreconstruction input from the particular OSforGFF construction examined here.

The analysis therefore distinguishes local proof warrant from inter-formalization warrant. The latter is not a substitute for proof checking; it concerns the mathematical correspondence required for a checked result to be transferred between formal representations. This distinction complements existing work on proof correspondence, interoperable scientific formalizations, mature mathematical formalisms, and cross-model warrant transfer. It also clarifies why the existence of separately verified components does not by itself establish the validity of a composed scientific inference.

The broader implication is that the epistemology of formalized mathematical physics must attend to the interfaces between formal developments as well as to the proofs contained within them. Machine checking can make those interfaces explicit and subject them to mathematical verification. The scientific significance of the resulting network nevertheless depends on what its formal representations preserve, which hypotheses are instantiated, and what kinds of physical claims are drawn from the reconstructed models. The reliability of a local proof and the warrant for its use across a scientific formalization network are related, but distinct, epistemic questions.
