# 4. Conclusion

This paper has examined what a machine-checked proof establishes when the formal system being verified is a selective formalization of a mathematical formalism used in physics. The Osterwalder–Schrader case indicates that the answer must be stated at more than one level. Kernel checking provides deductive warrant for a proposition relative to its formal definitions, assumptions, imports, and trust base. It does not, by itself, establish that the formal proposition captures every scientifically relevant feature of the source formalism, that a particular model satisfies the hypotheses of a conditional theorem, or that a result about the model warrants a claim about a physical target.

The three formalization projects illustrate these distinctions. OSforGFF establishes, within its formal setting, that the specified massive Gaussian free-field construction satisfies the project’s OS properties. OSreconstruction proves conditional forward and reverse reconstruction results, with the forward direction requiring the OS assumptions together with a separate arity-dependent growth condition. The Comparator project supplies an additional specification and proof-checking interface for the reconstruction statements. These results are mathematically substantial, but their scopes are not interchangeable. In particular, the OS1 regularity condition in OSforGFF is not definitionally identical to the growth condition required by OSreconstruction, and the public artifacts examined do not expose a single end-to-end theorem instantiating the latter condition from the former construction. The reconstruction package’s stated endpoint is a family of Wightman distributions, rather than a full GNS construction of a Hilbert space and operator-valued fields.

The philosophical significance of these distinctions can be expressed through the DDI framework. Machine checking strengthens demonstration: it makes selected assumptions and inferential steps explicit and subjects the proof object to formal checking. Denotation and interpretation remain separate. The formalization must correspond adequately to the relevant source formalism, and a model-internal result must be interpreted before it can support a target-directed inference. Where the scientific claim concerns an actual physical system, the adequacy of that inference depends on the representational relation, the purpose of the model, and the relevant theoretical or empirical evidence. This conclusion is consistent with existing accounts of surrogate reasoning and model evaluation, and it complements analyses of how rigorous mathematical relations can transmit empirical warrant between physical models (Gelfert 2009).

The argument does not imply that every formal result must culminate in an empirical claim. A machine-checked theorem may establish mathematical or modal knowledge about a model that functions as an epistemic artifact, without being used to represent a particular actual system. What matters is that the epistemic claim be identified correctly and assessed by the standards appropriate to it. Formal derivability, model-internal truth, explanatory understanding, and target-directed warrant are related achievements, but none should be treated as a substitute for the others.

The contribution of machine-checked proof in mathematical physics is therefore both significant and delimited. It strengthens the deductive warrant and auditability of demonstration within a formalized mathematical structure. It does not eliminate the interpretative and representational work through which that structure is connected to scientific practice and, where intended, to the physical world. The OS case makes this division of epistemic labour explicit: the proof assistant can certify what follows from the formalized assumptions, while the scientific significance of those assumptions, their realization in a model, and the scope of the resulting physical claims remain matters for mathematical and philosophical analysis.
