# 1. Introduction

The increasing use of interactive theorem provers in mathematical physics raises a question about the relation between formal proof and scientific knowledge. A proof assistant such as Lean can verify that a proposition follows from specified definitions and assumptions in a formal system. When a proof is accepted by the kernel, the resulting warrant is deductive: relative to the soundness of the system and its declared trust base, the proposition follows from the assumptions as they are represented in the formal language. This is a substantial achievement, particularly in areas of mathematical physics where arguments depend on long chains of functional-analytic estimates, distributional constructions, and transformations between different mathematical descriptions. Yet the scientific use of a checked theorem may involve several further steps. The formal statement must correspond to the relevant mathematical content of the physical theory; a concrete model must satisfy the theorem's hypotheses; and, where the result is used to make a claim about a physical target, the model-to-target inference must be justified. These relations become especially important when a theory is formalized in more than one mathematical representation.

The present paper investigates this problem through the Osterwalder–Schrader (OS) reconstruction programme. Its central question is: under what conditions can a machine-checked theorem in one formalization support an inference in another formalization of the same physical theory? The question concerns neither the reliability of Lean's kernel in isolation nor the general relation between mathematical models and reality. It concerns the interfaces through which deductive warrant is transferred between formal representations of scientific mathematics. We use the term *inter-formalization warrant* for the warrant supplied by an adequately established correspondence between such representations. The term is intended as an analytical distinction, not as the name of a new general theory of scientific knowledge.

The problem is related to, but not exhausted by, several existing discussions. Work on formalizing chemical physics has shown how Lean can make assumptions in scientific derivations explicit and how common structures can facilitate the reuse of results across theories. Bobbin et al. (2024), for example, develop a network of definitions for thermodynamics and kinematics and use it to establish interoperable proofs of gas laws and equations of motion. They also distinguish mathematical verification from the empirical adequacy of the theories being formalized. Their work establishes the practical importance of reusable formal structures; the present paper asks a different question about the warrant carried by correspondences between independently developed formal records and theorem interfaces. At a more abstract level, the formal-logical literature already provides a precise account of proof-preserving translations. Rabe (2017) defines theory morphisms as compositional translations between formal theories and proves judgment preservation: a derivation in the source theory yields a translated derivation in the target theory. Such morphisms also induce a corresponding model-reduction construction. This establishes the formal principle that theorem transfer can be justified by a proof-preserving interpretation. The present paper does not propose a new theory morphism or a new proof-transfer mechanism; it asks what additional epistemic work is required when the theories being related are formal representations of a physical formalism and the resulting mathematical claims are used in scientific reasoning.

Their concern is adjacent to a technical literature on proof transfer and heterogeneous formal reasoning. Al Wardani et al. (2023) develop the Distributed Assertion Management Framework, in which formal assertions can be exchanged with explicit provenance and configurable trust in their creators and tools. Guilloud et al. (2025) develop SC-TPTP to transfer and reconstruct proof derivations between automated and interactive theorem provers, including Lean. These projects establish that proof exchange, translation, and trust management are already active technical research areas. The present paper does not claim to introduce proof transfer or interoperability. It examines a different epistemic question: when the formal records being connected encode distinct mathematical presentations of a physical theory, what must be established about their mathematical and physical content before a checked result can support scientific inference?

This issue is particularly visible in recent work on the formalization of quantum field theory. Douglas, Hoback, et al. (2026) explain that the same physical ideas can be expressed in mathematically different ways and that formalization requires concrete choices influenced by the definitions and theorems already available in Lean libraries. Their formalization of the massive free field follows the Glimm–Jaffe probability-measure formulation of the OS axioms, rather than the original Schwinger-function formulation. They explicitly note that this is a variant of the original OS axioms and explain the relation between their use of Schwartz functions and the compactly supported test functions in the Glimm–Jaffe presentation. Their paper identifies the OS reconstruction theorem as a subsequent milestone for that development. The OSforGFF repository has since been extended to a dimension-generic result, but its mathematical representation remains the probability-measure and generating-functional formulation described by the authors.

Related work on autoformalization has identified a further source of difficulty. Meadows et al. (2026) characterize forms of semantic drift that occur when informal scientific statements are translated into Lean, including notational collapse and abstraction elevation. Their focus is the human-in-the-loop autoformalization of physics problems, whereas the present study concerns established mathematical-physics developments and their relations to one another. Nevertheless, their analysis reinforces the point that kernel correctness is a property of the formal statement actually encoded; it does not alone establish that the statement preserves the intended scientific content. The closest philosophical antecedent is DeDeo and Duede's (2026) analysis of correspondence between mathematical proofs and formal derivations. They distinguish adequate representation of the original theorem from tracking the inferential steps of the informal proof. Their discussion goes further than a simple translation problem: their account of library lock-in and shared models shows how inherited formal infrastructure can hybridize fragments from distinct modelling traditions, while their analysis of structural, causal, and explanatory routes identifies different kinds of evidence for correspondence. The present paper therefore does not claim to have discovered that library history shapes formal representation, or that machine checking alone settles semantic adequacy. It changes the unit of analysis from an informal proof and its formal derivation to two formal representations of mathematical-physical data. The question is whether objects, predicates, test-function domains, quantitative hypotheses, and output witnesses are related in a way that permits a theorem in the source representation to be used in the receiving one. This is a narrower, complementary interface problem; it does not replace their account of proof correspondence.

The philosophical background is provided by work on scientific representation and mathematical practice. Hughes's (1997) account of denotation, demonstration, and interpretation (DDI) distinguishes the representational relation between a model and its target from the reasoning performed within the model and the interpretation of the resulting conclusions (Hughes, 1997). Gelfert (2011, 2022), however, argues that the DDI and matching-model accounts each have shortcomings, and that scientific representation is often heterogeneous. Mature mathematical formalisms—physically interpreted systems of notation and locally applicable rules—can function as inferential and interpretative resources that are not reducible to a single representational relation. This point is important here: machine-checked proof should not be identified with scientific demonstration as a whole. It is one formalized component of reasoning conducted within a mathematical formalism. Gelfert's account of rigorous results and cross-model warrant transfer (2009) provides a further antecedent. It shows how mathematically established relations can transmit empirical warrant between physical models. The present paper does not claim that such transfer is new; it asks how formal proof and the interfaces between formalizations contribute to the deductive part of that process.

The distinction between local proof warrant and inter-formalization warrant is also consistent with the epistemological heterogeneity of computational science discussed by Imbert and Ardourel (2023). Verification can serve different epistemic roles depending on the object and purpose of the computational practice. A machine-checked proof may warrant a formal consequence, while correspondence with a source theory, instantiation in a model, and empirical assessment remain distinct tasks. The OS case makes these tasks unusually explicit because the projects expose their formal records, theorem hypotheses, test-function spaces, and proof-checking interfaces.

Three public Lean developments are examined. OSforGFF (Cherkis et al., 2026; commit fef84a82937dfd2bb7f672108a72da329d3ef0fd) constructs the massive Gaussian free field as a probability measure on tempered distributions and proves a dimension-generic Glimm–Jaffe package of OS properties. OSreconstruction (Yin et al., 2026; commit cf0904e34a7c146a9f312f601e3707dca4a87697) formalizes forward and reverse reconstruction results between Euclidean Schwinger data on a zero-diagonal Schwartz test-function space and Wightman-function distributions on the full Schwartz space. The OSreconstruction-comparator project (Douglas, Yin, et al., 2026; commit 93cdb40e728ba27b7b1b55efc30d57f7b43de9aa) supplies a Mathlib-only audit challenge that reproduces the production package's own audit definitions, together with explicit bridges between the separately elaborated audit records and the production records.

The case yields two contrasting findings at different interface levels. First, the Comparator provides a limited positive example of record-level interface warrant. Its bridges identify the audit and production OS records, preserve the Schwinger-function family, translate the growth hypotheses, and establish equivalence between the corresponding reconstruction propositions. This is not a bridge between independently authored physical formalisms: the challenge definitions reproduce the production package's own audit definitions, and the solution uses the production theorems through the proved adapters. The separate import and checking boundary makes the record correspondence auditable, but it does not independently validate the shared definitions as an adequate representation of the OS axioms or constitute a second derivation of the reconstruction mathematics. Second, the inspected public artifacts do not expose an end-to-end bridge from the particular OSforGFF probability-measure construction to the exact OS and growth-condition records required by OSreconstruction. The two projects use different mathematical records and test-function domains. In particular, OSforGFF's OS1 regularity condition is not the arity-dependent OS-II growth condition required by OSreconstruction. Although the latter package reports massive-free-field and Proca-Wick-square examples in a private companion project, that report does not identify the private massive-free-field inhabitant with the specific OSforGFF object at the pinned revision.

The paper's contribution is a source-critical analysis of the conditions under which results can be reused across formal representations in mathematical physics. It distinguishes (i) the local deductive warrant supplied by kernel checking, (ii) correspondence between a source physical formalism and its formal encoding, (iii) record-level interface warrant established by proved mappings between formal records, (iv) instantiation of theorem hypotheses by a concrete model, and (v) target-directed inference from a model to a physical system. These are not stages that every scientific claim must traverse in a single linear sequence. They are distinct relations that must be assessed whenever a claim relies on the composition of formalized results. The analysis does not propose a new general theory of scientific representation, proof correspondence, or empirical warrant transfer. Rather, it isolates the interface obligations that arise when machine-checked results are reused across distinct formal representations of a physical theory.

The method is a conceptual and source-critical case study of the theorem statements, definitions, bridge declarations, repository records, and original OS papers at fixed revisions. No new Lean implementation, numerical calculation, or independent proof replay is undertaken. The mathematical and formalization results are attributed to their authors, and repository-reported verification claims are distinguished from the source inspection performed here.
