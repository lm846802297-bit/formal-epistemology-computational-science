# In Lean We Trust? The Ideology of Machine-Checked Proofs in Science

**Project:** *In Lean We Trust?*  
**Version:** Stage 2 working manuscript — revised Abstract and Sections 1–3  
**Evidence cut-off:** 28 November 2026  
**Status:** Sections 1–3 revised; Sections 4–7 retained from the seven-section baseline pending Stages 3–4.

## Abstract

Formal verification does not become ideological because its proofs are unreliable. Lean’s kernel supplies genuine, bounded warrant by checking a proof term against a specified formal proposition within a declared environment. The question is how this local warrant can acquire authority beyond the proposition checked as the credential “Lean-verified” circulates through papers, repositories, institutional announcements, and benchmark records. Drawing on Marx and Žižek, the article examines how distributed epistemic labour is condensed into a portable credential and how authority generated by technical, semantic, and institutional relations can appear to reside in the certified object. Ideological fantasy stages closure around a wider mathematical claim; fetishism displaces the relations sustaining that claim into the credential; fetishistic disavowal names the conditional persistence of practical investment after mediation has been acknowledged. The article introduces **Registry II** as a diagnostic ideal type and institutional risk threshold: a repeatable registration logic that stabilizes machine-centred attribution through standardized metrics and records, persists across contexts despite acknowledged mediation or revised classification, and carries consequences for recognition or credibility allocation. Four cases—Riemann, Navier–Stokes, AlphaProof Nexus, and Liquid Tensor—trace a spectrum from transparent certification to public credit compression. They do not establish that Registry II is already fully institutionalized. The article offers a conditional Žižekian diagnostic of how genuine technical warrant may be enlarged into symbolic and institutional authority.

**Keywords:** philosophy of science; mathematical practice; ideology critique; formal verification; Lean; epistemic authority

## 1. The Paradox of a Verified Proof

What could be ideological about a correct proof?

Imagine that Lean’s kernel accepts a proof term for a specified formal proposition. The system has performed the task for which it was designed. The result is reproducible and, in principle, independently checkable. Why, then, should ideology enter the analysis? Would this not confuse a genuine mathematical achievement with a social illusion?

The paradox depends on reducing ideology to false consciousness. Žižek’s critique unsettles that reduction: a statement may be factually correct and still participate in an ideological relation through the position it occupies and the practices it sustains (Žižek, 1989, 1994). The question is not whether Lean’s verdict is correct, but what that verdict is made to authorize.

A kernel check establishes a proof of a formal proposition under a declared environment. A claim that a mathematical problem has been conclusively solved may also depend on whether the formal proposition captures the intended theorem, whether the result is new, how the achievement should be attributed, and what status it has within mathematical practice. These questions can be answered, but they are not all answered by the kernel’s local operation. The credential “Lean-verified” can nevertheless travel between these levels of claim.

The central question is: under what conditions does the genuine but bounded warrant of Lean verification acquire an ideological function as an apparently self-sufficient guarantee of broader mathematical authority? The object of analysis is not Lean software in general or a generalized psychology of trust in machines. It is the public and institutional credential “Lean-verified” and the record chains through which it circulates.

The article introduces **Registry II** to name a further institutional risk. Registry II is a diagnostic ideal type, not the name of an existing database or a claim that every current case already instantiates a completed system. It describes a repeatable registration logic in which machine-centred attribution is standardized through metrics and records, persists across contexts despite acknowledged mediation or revised classification, and acquires consequences for recognition, credit, ranking, or credibility allocation. The issue is not simply that a database contains an inaccurate field. It is whether a record becomes a durable institutional representation of who or what accomplished a mathematical result, and whether that representation continues to organize recognition after its qualifications have changed.

Such a risk can arise through ordinary institutional pressures rather than deliberate deception. Competitive AI research and public communication reward concise demonstrations of capability; benchmarks require standardized units that can be counted and compared; and downstream summaries or citations can reproduce a compact metric while dropping qualifications about variants, prior results, or human contributions. These mechanisms do not automatically produce fetishism. They identify conditions under which credit compression may become institutionally stabilized.

The four cases occupy different positions along this spectrum. Riemann and Liquid Tensor show certification in which mathematical review and semantic mediation remain visible. Navier–Stokes raises questions of formal correspondence and the attribution of upstream research. AlphaProof Nexus presents a more concentrated form of public credit compression. The cases test the Registry II threshold rather than presupposing that any one of them establishes it. Section 2 specifies what Lean verification warrants; Section 3 develops the Marxian and Žižekian account of fantasy, fetishism, and practical belief; Sections 4–6 reconstruct and compare the mechanism across the cases; and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. The Limits of Formal Certainty

### 2.1 The kernel’s warrant

Lean is a proof assistant based on dependent type theory. A proposition is represented as a formal type, and a proof is represented by a term inhabiting that type. The kernel checks whether the term has the required type relative to a declared formal environment, including definitions, axioms, imported libraries, and the trusted implementation. Kernel acceptance therefore provides genuine derivational warrant: the proof term is accepted as establishing the specified formal proposition under the stated environment.

This is a substantial epistemic achievement. It can exclude many classes of derivational error, support replay of proof artifacts, and permit independent checking by separately implemented kernels or proof checkers. The fact that an opaque AI system generated a proof does not by itself undermine the warrant supplied by a transparent checker. Duede and Davey (2025), for example, argue that a transparent proof checker can provide genuine mathematical knowledge even when the process that generated the proof is opaque. The check is not merely a performance for an audience. It does real epistemic work.

### 2.2 The wider warrant architecture

Kernel acceptance concerns a proof of a formal proposition. The relation between that proposition and the intended mathematical claim is a distinct specification task. It asks whether the formal definitions, assumptions, and statement capture the mathematical question that researchers intend to address. This relation can be scrutinized with great care: source statements can be compared, definitions inspected, and formalizations assessed by mathematicians. In some settings, parts of the specification relation can themselves be formalized. The point is not that formal and informal mathematics are separated by an unbridgeable gulf. It is that establishing correspondence is distinct from checking the proof term.

This component-based understanding of mathematical warrant is already developed in the literature. Vallverdú’s Locus Thesis argues that properties such as reliability, surveyability, transferability, and explanatory power belong to components of a warrant architecture rather than being indiscriminately attributed to a proof as a whole (Vallverdú, 2026). Kallel and El Louadi (2026) distinguish derivational validity, representational fidelity, and epistemic significance, showing why abundant machine checking does not remove the need for human adjudication. The present article adopts this work as its technical baseline; it does not claim originality for the distinction between a formal proposition, a proof object, a checking procedure, a trusted base, and an intended mathematical claim.

Other questions remain distinct. A proof can be derivationally valid while the result is not new, because an earlier theorem already entails it. A formal statement can faithfully represent a conjecture while credit for selecting, formulating, proving, or interpreting it remains distributed among different contributors. Mathematical understanding, institutional recognition, and downstream use also require separate evidence.

### 2.3 Boundary conditions: reliance, mediation, and ideology

These distinctions establish the boundary of the argument. Reliance on Lean, expert formalizers, or independent checkers can be rational. Scientific knowledge depends on a division of epistemic labour, and Hardwig’s account of epistemic dependence explains why it can be reasonable to rely on experts whose evidence one cannot personally reproduce (Hardwig, 1985). De Millo, Lipton, and Perlis (1979) likewise show that mathematical proof operates through social processes of communication, criticism, and acceptance. Distributed labour, institutional recognition, technical reuse, and concise public communication are not, by themselves, evidence of ideology.

The analysis therefore does not treat kernel reliability as illusory, equate social mediation with fetishism, or infer private motives from public wording. The ideological question arises only when a credential’s bounded warrant is extended to a wider claim, when the authority generated by the relations supporting that claim appears to reside autonomously in the credential, and when this displacement has a recognizable practical or institutional effect. Registry II sets a further threshold: it concerns the stabilization of such an attribution in repeatable records and metrics, its persistence across contexts, and its consequences for recognition or credibility. These conditions are not presumed from the use of formal verification. They are the standards against which the cases are assessed.

## 3. Žižek and the Ideological Structure of Verification

### 3.1 Ideology beyond false consciousness

In “The Spectre of Ideology,” Žižek shifts the analysis of ideology beyond the opposition between true and false beliefs (Žižek, 1994). A statement may be factually correct and still participate in an ideological relation through the position it occupies and the practices it sustains. “Lean checked this proof” may be entirely accurate; the question is what wider authority that true statement is made to carry. Ideology concerns the relation between what is said, what is materially done, and what becomes authoritative.

### 3.2 Ideological fantasy: the scene of closure

In *The Sublime Object of Ideology*, fantasy sustains the appearance of coherence where a constitutive gap remains (Žižek, 1989, pp. 28–31). In formal verification, the relevant distinction is between the formal proposition checked by the kernel and the intended mathematical claim to which it is connected. Their correspondence can be rigorously established, but it remains a distinct task from kernel acceptance.

Fantasy names the scene in which the local verdict appears to complete the wider work of specification, interpretation, and institutional assessment. The certificate seems to settle not only whether the formal proposition has been proved, but whether it is the intended theorem, whether the result is novel, and whether the mathematical dispute is over. The word “verified” gives a heterogeneous process a single point of presentation; fantasy explains how completion of this local task can appear as completion of the whole.

### 3.3 Fetishism: when the relation appears as a property of the object

Marx’s account of commodity fetishism analyzes how relations among producers assume the appearance of relations among things; Žižek emphasizes how an effect generated by a network of relations can appear to reside autonomously in an object (Marx, 1976/1867, Vol. 1, Chapter 1, Section 4; Žižek, 1989, pp. 19–20). The analogy with formal verification is structural, not literal. Lean is not a commodity in Marx’s technical economic sense, and mathematical truth is not produced by social labour. The relevant structure is the appearance of relation-dependent authority as an intrinsic property of an object.

The authority of a Lean-verified result depends on problem formulation, formal specification, proof construction, libraries and software maintenance, kernel checking, semantic comparison, expert scrutiny, and institutional recognition. Fetishism appears when this network-generated authority seems to reside in the credential itself. The network makes the credential meaningful, yet the credential appears as the source that certifies the network. Fantasy stages closure; fetishism gives that closure an objectal form.

Thomas et al. (2018) offer a close precedent. Their ethnography treats algorithms as material objects that stabilize social relations and carry promises generated through exchanges among makers and users; it traces the slippage between technical capabilities and broader claims without reducing fetishism to false consciousness. The present article does not claim to introduce algorithmic fetishism. Its narrower object is a credential grounded in a genuine kernel check, and its question is how that local warrant can be made to stand for a wider mathematical claim.

### 3.4 Fetishistic disavowal and Registry II

Žižek’s account of fetishistic belief directs attention to practices rather than explicit propositions. Subjects may know that money expresses social relations while continuing to treat it in practice as the embodiment of wealth; belief is externalized in objects and procedures (Žižek, 1989, pp. 28–32). This differs from cynical disavowal, which concerns awareness of the distance between an ideological mask and social reality while continuing to insist on the mask (Sloterdijk, 1987; Žižek, 1989, pp. 24–27). Black (2025) clarifies the distinction. Here, fetishistic disavowal is a conditional public or institutional mechanism: acknowledged mediation coexists with a practical investment in machine-centred authority. It is not presumed in every formalization and does not imply private or unconscious motives.

I use **Registry II** to name the institutionalized ideal type toward which this mechanism can tend. It is not a particular database or a mistaken field in a data schema. It is a second-order registration logic that standardizes who or what solved a problem, how many results were achieved, and what status attaches to the solver. Its records may take the form of benchmark leaderboards, research metrics, press-release formulas, institutional news items, or downstream citations. What matters is the rule of attribution that these records stabilize.

Three structural pressures make this risk intelligible. Competitive AI research and public communication reward concise, comparable demonstrations of capability; scientific priority and recognition systems make solver-centred claims legible as signs of achievement (Bourdieu, 2004; Merton, 1957, 1968). Benchmark leaderboards further require standardized counts, ranks, denominators, and success categories, which can suppress distinctions among original problems, variants, corrected statements, prior results, and human contributions. Finally, records travel: software identifiers, citation practices, and dependency records make artifacts retrievable, but can also allow an earlier metric or attribution to be repeated after its qualifications have changed (Druskat, 2020; Smith et al., 2016). These are structural pressures, not proof that every leaderboard or press release performs an ideological function.

Registry II is therefore a diagnostic ideal type and institutional test threshold. Its presence would require evidence that: (1) human, semantic, or classificatory mediation has been acknowledged, or the original achievement category has been revised; (2) a standardized record nevertheless preserves machine-centred attribution or the earlier aggregate metric; (3) that representation persists across connected public or institutional contexts; and (4) the persistence has a recognizable consequence for ranking, credit, recognition, credibility, or resource allocation. A mistaken entry that is promptly corrected is a local data error, not Registry II. A press release that compresses a collaboration is evidence of credit compression, but not by itself institutionalized disavowal. The stronger diagnosis requires persistence and consequence.

Lacanian concepts clarify the symbolic dimension without turning an institution or software system into a psychoanalytic subject. The Big Other names the symbolic locus of norms and recognition, not a particular university, journal, or database; the subject-supposed-to-know names a position attributed within a transferential relation, not a synonym for trust in a tool (Lacan, 1977, pp. 230–235; Lacan, 2019, Chapter 16). A solver or credential may be installed in a knowing position when its output is treated as carrying authority beyond the task it performed. Registry II would stabilize that attribution in repeatable records. The four cases test this threshold rather than confirm it in advance: the full diagnosis depends on the persistence of records and their consequences, not on the mere presence of a machine-generated proof or an autonomous-solver phrase.

## 4. The Machine as a Fetish Object

The ideological function of formal verification can be reconstructed through five connected moments. They are analytical distinctions, not a chronology through which every formalization must pass. The first three explain how a genuine technical achievement becomes a portable credential; the fourth identifies the specifically fetishistic conversion; the fifth describes a conditional feedback mechanism through which the credential’s authority may be reproduced after its conditions have been acknowledged.

**First, the check has real technical efficacy.** Lean’s kernel checks whether a proof term has the required type for a specified formal proposition under a declared environment. This is not a symbolic performance. The proof can be replayed, inspected, and, where appropriate, checked by independent implementations. The credential has epistemic force because the operation it records is genuinely reliable within its stated scope.

**Second, the achievement depends on distributed epistemic labour.** Problem selection, mathematical formulation, formal specification, proof construction, library and software maintenance, checking, semantic comparison, expert scrutiny, and exposition contribute different forms of warrant. No single component performs the whole task. This distribution is a normal condition of contemporary mathematics, not a defect. It becomes relevant to fetishism when the authority generated through these relations is later represented as if it belonged to one object.

**Third, communication condenses this work into a credential.** A complex result must travel through papers, abstracts, repositories, institutional announcements, benchmark records, and later citations. “Lean-verified” is a compact and often useful way of communicating that a formal proposition has passed a specified check. Condensation is not itself ideological. A concise description can preserve the scope of the result and acknowledge the work that connects the formal statement to the intended theorem.

**Fourth, objectal displacement occurs when the credential appears to contain the authority of the whole.** As the sign travels, the formal proposition, environment, assumptions, specification relation, and human assessment may become less visible, while the credential remains. Authority generated by the network then appears as an autonomous property of the machine-certified object. The network makes the credential meaningful, yet the credential appears to certify the network. Fantasy stages the appearance that the wider question has been closed; fetishism gives that closure an objectal form.

**Fifth, fetishistic disavowal can reproduce this authority after mediation has been acknowledged.** In Žižek’s account, practical belief need not coincide with an explicit proposition in an individual’s mind. It can be externalized in objects and procedures. Applied here, the mechanism is present when a public or institutional practice acknowledges human, semantic, or classificatory mediation but continues to organize attribution around the machine as the apparent bearer of the whole achievement. This is a conditional feedback mechanism, not a necessary feature of formal verification. It does not license an inference about private or unconscious motives.

### Registry II: from credential to institutional record

**Registry II** names the institutionalized ideal type toward which this feedback mechanism can tend. The distinction is between a first-order record that a formal proposition has been checked and a second-order record that registers who or what solved a mathematical problem, how many results were achieved, and what status should attach to the solver. Registry II is not a particular database and is not reducible to a mistaken field in a data schema. It is a repeatable rule of attribution that can be embodied in benchmark leaderboards, research metrics, press-release formulas, institutional news items, and downstream citations.

Three structural pressures make this risk intelligible. First, competitive AI research and public-relations incentives reward concise, comparable demonstrations of capability. A distributed research process can therefore be compressed into a solver-centred claim that is legible as evidence of model performance. Second, benchmark leaderboards require standardized units—counts, ranks, denominators, and success categories. Aggregation can suppress distinctions among original problems, variants, corrected statements, prior results, and different kinds of human contribution. Third, public records travel. Once a metric or attribution enters a paper, institutional page, repository, or later summary, citation and copying practices can reproduce it without rechecking the original statement, proof file, novelty status, or credit allocation. These pressures explain why Registry II is a structural risk rather than merely an isolated database error; they do not establish that every leaderboard or press release performs an ideological function.

Registry II is therefore a **diagnostic ideal type and institutional test threshold**. Its presence requires evidence of four conditions:

1. Human, semantic, or classificatory mediation has been publicly acknowledged, or the original achievement category has been revised.
2. A standardized record nevertheless preserves machine-centred attribution or an earlier aggregate metric.
3. The representation persists across connected public or institutional contexts rather than appearing in one isolated statement.
4. Its persistence has a recognizable consequence for ranking, credit, recognition, credibility, or resource allocation.

A press release that compresses a collaboration is evidence of credit compression, not by itself Registry II. A mistaken entry that is promptly corrected is a local data error. A published audit that challenges a metric creates a counter-record, but does not alone establish that the older metric continues to organize institutional action. The stronger diagnosis requires persistence and consequence. The four cases below are assessed against this threshold; none is presumed to instantiate it in full.

## 5. Four Encounters with Formal Verification

The cases are compared as different configurations of formal validity, specification, attribution, and public recognition. In the table below, **V** refers only to kernel acceptance of the relevant formal proposition in the identified artifact and environment. It does not certify novelty, priority, authorship, the fidelity of a separate natural-language proof, or institutional acceptance. The documentary snapshot is **28 November 2026**.

### 5.1 The Riemann Hypothesis: formal certification and layered recognition

On 20 November 2026, Levent Alpöge released a Lean development and a short note describing his work with Anthropic’s Claude (Alpöge, 2026). The note does not present the result as a human working alone with an automated checker. Alpöge describes an asymmetric collaboration: he guided the project toward the relevant mathematical setting, while Claude supplied decisive technical ideas near the end, including a suggested length for a chain of theta lifts. This is more specific than the general description of the proof as AI-assisted.

The Lean FRO and Mathlib Initiative issued a joint statement on 21 November expressing full confidence in the proof and certifying both the formal statement and the proof. The statement reports expert vetting of the Mathlib definition, comparison of the theorem statement with the archive, compilation, and acceptance by Lean’s kernel and independent checkers (Lean FRO & Mathlib Initiative, 2026; Sutherland, 2026a). The credential therefore rests on a visibly composite warrant: formal verification, statement review, and independent checking.

The institutional layer is distinct. As of 28 November, the Clay Mathematics Institute’s Riemann Hypothesis page labels the problem “Active” (Clay Mathematics Institute, n.d.). That status is not an award and does not itself constitute acceptance of a proposed solution. Under CMI’s published prize rules, a proposed solution can be considered only after publication in a qualifying outlet, the passage of at least two years, and general acceptance in the global mathematical community (Clay Mathematics Institute, 2018). The case therefore separates strong formal-community certification from the separate institutional process governing Millennium Prize recognition.

Riemann is not an example of a machine credential wholly detached from its warrant. The joint statement makes the layers of checking visible, while the continuing work of exposition and mathematical reconstruction remains part of the public record. Its comparative role is to show that strong institutional and community recognition can coexist with an explicit account of the human, semantic, and technical relations supporting the credential.

### 5.2 Navier–Stokes: proof correspondence and the politics of credit

On 8 September 2026, OpenAI announced a result for the Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem, presenting an analytical write-up alongside a Lean formalization (OpenAI, 2026a). The announcement describes an internal multi-agent process and states that the Lean formalization and verification followed the system’s resolution. The formal artifact is a genuine checked object; the separate question is how it corresponds to the written mathematical argument.

Bastounis, Circelli, and Hansen (2026) identify differences between the paper and the Lean declarations, including the derivative count in the inverse estimate of Lemma 8.6 and a different pressure-flux estimate and proof route. Their criticism concerns paper-to-Lean correspondence. It does not show that the kernel accepted an invalid proof or establish that the formal theorem is false. The source-level comparison and the kernel verdict are distinct forms of evidence.

A second tension concerns priority and attribution in the surrounding research. In his signed statement, Tristan Buckmaster describes a personal collaboration with Levent Alpöge, involving Claude and Codex, that produced results for the forced Euler and Boussinesq equations. He credits the underlying research programme to Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa, whose earlier work supplied the starting point for the construction (Buckmaster, 2026). Buckmaster also recounts discussions with OpenAI about concurrent publication and authorship. He explicitly states that he had not seen OpenAI’s proof and did not know whether his and Alpöge’s data had been used; his statement is an account of those conversations, not an adjudication of the underlying claims.

OpenAI’s public account acknowledges the priority of Buckmaster and Alpöge’s work on forced Euler while distinguishing their forced Euler result from OpenAI’s unforced Euler result. It presents the Navier–Stokes result as a separate result and denies that the researchers or agents accessed their work before public release (OpenAI, 2026a). The public record therefore contains a dispute over credit, priority, and the presentation of related research, but it does not establish that the parties proved the same theorem or that data were misused. In particular, Buckmaster’s statement about unpublished work on hypo-dissipative Navier–Stokes must not be conflated with the published forced Euler results or with OpenAI’s claimed forced Navier–Stokes result.

The case thus has two analytically distinct dimensions: a correspondence problem between a checked formal artifact and a separate written proof, and a credit problem involving the attribution of upstream mathematical ideas and the contributions of human collaborators and AI systems. It illustrates how a Lean credential can accompany a high-profile solution claim while both the proof route and the allocation of research credit remain objects of public scrutiny. Neither dimension by itself establishes Registry II.

### 5.3 Erdős problems and AlphaProof Nexus: a valid proof and a changed novelty classification

Tsoukalas and colleagues report that AlphaProof Nexus “autonomously resolved 9 of 353 open Erdős problems” (Tsoukalas et al., 2026). The nine entries in Table 1 are formalized statements associated with seven problem numbers; they include parts and variants, and the #26 entry is a Tenenbaum variant rather than an original problem posed by Erdős. The paper also describes human contributions to problem selection, statement formulation, validation of the correspondence between Lean statements and original conjectures, natural-language exposition, and repository integration.

Erdős problem #846 provides a particularly clear distinction between formal validity and mathematical novelty. The AlphaProof Nexus Lean proof gives a negative answer to the problem and passed the project’s formal checks. The point raised by the later audit is not that the proof is invalid. It is that the negative answer was already implied by published mathematics. Reiher, Rödl, and Sales (2024, Theorem 1.5) establish a multidimensional colouring-versus-density result: for a finite configuration (F), they construct a set in which every finite colouring contains a monochromatic homothetic copy of (F), while every finite subset contains a proportionally large (F)-free subset. Specializing (F) to a collinear triple in the plane gives the two properties needed for a counterexample to #846: every finite subset contains a large subset with no three collinear, while no finite partition can consist entirely of sets with no three collinear. The Erdős Problems entry for #846 records Rödl’s observation that the negative answer follows from the Reiher–Rödl–Sales result after a suitable projection (Erdős Problems, n.d.).

Hedegreen’s audit, *Nine Became Eight*, therefore removes #846 from a strict count of newly resolved open problems while retaining the validity of the formal proof. It reports eight newly resolved problems out of 353 under its novelty criterion, rather than the nine reported in the AlphaProof paper (Hedegreen, 2026). The audit is a public classification review, not a peer-reviewed mathematical correction; its author explicitly limits the claim and does not present the work as an independent specialist verification of every proof. The mathematical basis for the #846 reclassification is the earlier published theorem and the problem record, not the authority of the audit alone.

The distinction is consequential. The Science paper’s nine-of-353 figure remains its reported result; eight-of-353 is an external reclassification under a stricter novelty rule, not an official correction to the paper’s statistic. The Lean proof of #846 can remain valid as a proof of its formal target while the claim that it newly solved an open problem changes category. This is the central example in the case: formal verification establishes the proof of the encoded statement, but does not determine whether the result was already implied by prior mathematics.

The public record also contains a secondary provenance problem: the paper’s supplementary link for #741(ii) points to a file for #12(ii), while the appropriate #741 proof is recorded elsewhere. This is a source-link and artifact-identification issue, not a kernel failure. It reinforces the need to match each public claim to the exact formal file, but it is not the main novelty issue illustrated by #846.

AlphaProof Nexus is therefore the strongest case of public credit compression in the four-case comparison. The machine’s formal proof search is real, and human validation is explicitly reported; the headline count nevertheless aggregates formal statements, parts, variants, and results with different novelty status into a single capability metric. The #846 audit creates a public counter-record to the nine-result count. This is a **Registry II candidate**, not a completed diagnosis: the record shows a discrepancy between the benchmark metric and a public novelty reclassification, but does not by itself establish that the original metric was knowingly retained across all relevant institutional contexts or that its persistence caused a further allocation of credit or resources.

### 5.4 The Liquid Tensor Experiment: semantic auditability as a counter-practice

The Liquid Tensor Experiment was completed in July 2022, with the main theorem formally verified in Lean and a large collaborative contribution from mathematicians, formalizers, library maintainers, and technical contributors (Mathlib Community, 2022). Its public Blueprint keeps the informal exposition, formal declarations, and dependency structure navigable.

The project’s response to the semantic question is particularly important. Adam Topaz’s discussion of the experiment asks how mathematicians can know that the definitions introduced in Lean capture the intended objects. A pathological definition—for example, one that makes an Ext construction trivial—could allow a formally valid theorem to say very little about the intended mathematics. The project’s examples folder supplies sanity checks that mathematicians can inspect, making the semantic adequacy of the definitions an explicit part of the public warrant (Topaz, 2022).

The later Carleson formalization explicitly traces its use of definition-sanity checks to the Liquid Tensor Experiment and adopts Blueprint tooling to keep informal mathematics and formal declarations aligned (Becker et al., 2026). This is methodological uptake, not direct reuse of the LTE theorem or proof code. The case therefore provides a counter-practice: the formal credential remains important, but the semantic work that gives it mathematical meaning is made inspectable and is carried forward as a reusable method.

## 6. The Ideological Function of Mathematical Certainty

The cases reveal that the authority of formal verification is neither located in the kernel alone nor detached from it. The kernel supplies a real technical warrant. The mathematical and institutional practices surrounding it make that warrant meaningful and allow it to travel. The ideological question concerns the transformation through which the authority of this wider network comes to appear as a property of the machine-certified object.

The first three moments establish the material and communicative conditions of this transformation. A formal proof is genuinely checked; its production depends on distributed epistemic labour; and scientific communication condenses this labour into a portable credential. These moments explain why “Lean-verified” is credible and why it can circulate. They do not, by themselves, amount to fetishism. The decisive transition occurs when the authority generated by the network appears to reside autonomously in the credential. Condensation makes the result portable; objectal displacement makes the portable sign appear to contain the authority of the whole.

The four encounters illuminate different sides of this transition. Riemann shows how a strong institutional credential can remain attached to a visibly composite warrant. Navier–Stokes makes the relation between a formal declaration and a public solution claim an explicit problem of interpretation. Liquid Tensor shows how semantic scrutiny can remain visible within the practice that supports confidence in a formal result. These cases clarify the conditions under which the credential’s authority is assembled, communicated, and kept connected to the work that sustains it.

AlphaProof Nexus establishes the existential claim concerning the fifth moment. The *Science* paper reports that experts validated the correspondence between Lean statements and original Erdős conjectures; its author-contribution statement records human roles in problem selection, validation of generated solutions, and writing natural-language proofs. Aarhus University’s institutional account likewise identifies human problem selection, solution validation, and exposition, yet retains the autonomous-resolution framing in its headline and opening description. The same public communication chain therefore acknowledges human and semantic mediation while continuing to present the machine as the apparent autonomous subject of the mathematical achievement.

The distinction between operational and symbolic autonomy is crucial. Operationally, the agent searches for and generates a proof of a formalized target without a mathematician constructing each proof step. Symbolically, “autonomously resolved open problems” functions as the public attribution of the mathematical achievement, including the selection and interpretation of the target, validation of its correspondence to the original conjecture, and production of an intelligible mathematical result. The sources identify human work in these latter tasks. The narrower truth of autonomous proof search thus becomes the support for a wider representation of autonomous mathematical discovery. The ideological effect lies in this coexistence: human mediation is publicly acknowledged, yet the machine remains the apparent bearer of the achievement.

The three diagnostic dimensions make the argument explicit. **Scope extension, in its agency-attribution form,** occurs when autonomy in formal proof search is publicly attributed as autonomy in resolving the mathematical problem itself. The point is not that the mathematical result lacks warrant: the paper reports expert validation of the correspondence between the formal statements and the original conjectures. The extension concerns the attribution of agency from one operation within the research process to the problem-level achievement. **Objectal displacement** occurs when the machine is presented as the autonomous subject and bearer of the achievement, while human problem selection, statement validation, and mathematical exposition are acknowledged but positioned around the machine’s central act of resolution. **Social efficacy** is visible in the role of this formulation in the *Science* abstract and table and in the university’s official headline and institutional report. The case therefore satisfies the article’s existential claim at the level of public institutional representation. This does not require a claim about the authors’ private intentions or the beliefs of every reader.

The fifth moment is therefore not a claim that participants are ignorant of the human work involved. It is the opposite. The case shows that acknowledgement does not necessarily dissolve symbolic investment. The machine’s autonomy at the level of proof search can remain the public image through which the whole achievement is represented. **The ideological fetishism of formal verification does not require the concealment of human mediation; it can operate through the very public acknowledgement of that mediation.** This is the article’s central philosophical finding, established as an existence claim rather than a claim about every formalization project.

The distinction between the **warrant of a check** and the **authority of a credential** provides the conceptual hinge. The warrant concerns what the formal system establishes. The credential’s authority concerns how that result is recognized, circulated, and made to count. The credential is grounded in a real technical achievement, but its symbolic authority is produced through the relations that connect the formal artifact to the intended mathematical claim and through the public forms in which the achievement is represented. The machine’s reliability supplies the material support for this authority; it does not by itself explain the authority’s wider social meaning.

This argument enters into conversation with recent work on mathematical warrant and AI-assisted proof. Vallverdú’s Locus Thesis provides a component-based account of mathematical warrant (Vallverdú, 2026). Kallel and El Louadi (2026) distinguish derivational validity, representational fidelity, and epistemic significance, while examining the relation between abundant checking and human adjudication. López-Corona’s account of “studio mathematics” analyzes human-machine assemblages, provenance, agency, and responsibility in AI-assisted proof production (López-Corona, 2026). These works clarify how mathematical warrant is distributed across technical and human practices. The present article adds a Žižekian account of how the authority generated through those practices can become condensed in a portable credential and continue to function as autonomous machine authority even when the human conditions of its production are visible.

Erfani (2026) examines ideological functions of AI systems, while Hourigan (2009) applies Žižek to technological fetishization and knowledge production. Omodeo (2019) develops a political epistemology of ideology in science studies. Against this background, the present article focuses on the specific credential “Lean-verified.” Its contribution is not the general claim that technology can become fetishized, nor the discovery that mathematical knowledge depends on collective labour. It is the reconstruction of a particular mechanism through which a genuinely reliable proof-checking operation becomes the material support for a wider symbolic authority: real technical reliability, distributed epistemic labour, credential condensation, objectal displacement, and the reproduction of symbolic investment through fetishistic disavowal. The phrase “epistemic fetishism” has also been used in a different analytic-epistemological sense by Ahlstrom-Vij (2021); the present article therefore uses the full expression “the ideological fetishism of formal verification” and does not claim to have coined the bare term.

The ideological function of mathematical certainty is not that certainty is false. Formal verification can provide a real and powerful form of certainty. The issue is how this certainty travels and what it is made to authorize. The machine’s reliability is not what ideology must explain away. It is precisely what ideology must explain: how a genuine, bounded technical guarantee becomes the material support for a symbolic authority that appears to exceed the operation which produced it.

## 7. Conclusion

How does the epistemic fetishism of formal verification arise? It begins with a real technical achievement. Lean’s kernel checks proof terms against formal propositions under specified environments. This genuine reliability gives the credential “Lean-verified” its initial force; without it, the credential would have no epistemic substance.

The authority of the credential is assembled through distributed epistemic labour. Mathematical formulation, formal specification, proof construction, library and software maintenance, kernel checking, semantic comparison, expert assessment, and institutional recognition contribute different forms of warrant. Scientific communication condenses this process into a portable sign. As the credential circulates, the relations that give it meaning can recede while the sign remains. Authority generated by the network then appears to reside autonomously in the machine-certified object. This is the fetishistic displacement at the centre of the argument.

AlphaProof Nexus shows how this authority can persist through the public acknowledgement of human mediation. The *Science* paper reports expert validation of the correspondence between Lean statements and original Erdős conjectures and records human contributions to problem selection, solution validation, and natural-language proofs. Aarhus University’s institutional account identifies the same human work, yet retains the description of the system as having autonomously resolved the problems. The machine remains the apparent public bearer of the achievement alongside an explicit account of the human work that makes the result meaningful; the issue is the attribution of agency, not the validity of the results. This is fetishistic disavowal as a structure of public representation: acknowledgement does not automatically dissolve symbolic investment.

The other cases clarify the range of the mechanism. Riemann shows how institutional certification can rest on a visibly composite warrant. Navier–Stokes exposes the significance of the relation between a checked formal declaration and the natural-language proof associated with it. Liquid Tensor shows how semantic scrutiny and collective mathematical labour can remain visible within a formalization project. Their differences make clear that the same technical reliability can enter distinct social and symbolic arrangements.

The article’s central contribution is the concept of the **ideological fetishism of formal verification**: the transformation through which the socially and semantically mediated authority of a machine-checked result appears to reside autonomously in the credential “Lean-verified.” The five-moment account explains how this transformation is formed and reproduced: real technical reliability, distributed epistemic labour, credential condensation, objectal displacement, and fetishistic disavowal. Its decisive philosophical finding is that the fetish does not depend on hiding the human relations that sustain the machine’s authority. It can persist through their public acknowledgement.

The machine’s reliability is not what ideology must explain away. It is precisely what ideology must explain: how a real technical guarantee becomes the material support for a symbolic authority that exceeds the operation which produced it.

The machine verifies a proposition. The philosophical question is what, beyond that proposition, we ask the credential to guarantee.
