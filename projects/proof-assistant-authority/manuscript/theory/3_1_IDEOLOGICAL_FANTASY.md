# 3.1 The Ideological Fantasy of Scientific Certainty

**Status:** theory-chapter research draft; citations and wording remain subject to final manuscript editing.

## 1. The social problem of inaccessible knowledge

Modern societies depend on scientific claims whose evidential basis is not directly accessible to most of those who rely on them. A citizen may need to make decisions in relation to medicine, climate, energy, public health, or technological risk without possessing the training, instruments, time, or institutional access required to reproduce the relevant research. This is not simply a deficit of public knowledge. It is a structural feature of a specialized division of epistemic labour.

The relevant question is therefore not why ordinary people fail to verify everything for themselves. No individual could do so. The question is how a society makes the reliability of specialized knowledge publicly legible when direct inspection is unavailable. Scientific trust is mediated through experts, institutions, procedures, and communicative forms. Hardwig's account of epistemic dependence clarifies why such reliance can be rational: a layperson may have good reason to believe that an expert has good reasons for a claim without possessing or understanding the expert's evidence (Hardwig 1985, 338–40). The dependence is not an accidental weakness of the public; it is part of the conditions under which complex knowledge is possible.

Lean enters this social problem in a distinctive way. It transforms a mathematical proof into a formal object whose derivation can be checked by a small trusted kernel. This provides a real and valuable form of assurance: relative to a specified formal proposition, definitions, axioms, and computing environment, the proof term is accepted or rejected by a reproducible procedure. The procedure makes one part of the epistemic process unusually visible. A public audience need not inspect every line of a proof to understand the significance of a claim that a formal checker has accepted it.

The ideological question begins not with the existence of this technical assurance, but with the symbolic role it may acquire when it circulates through institutions and public communication.

## 2. Žižek: fantasy is not simply a false belief

In *The Sublime Object of Ideology*, Žižek's account of ideological fantasy cannot be reduced to wishful thinking, propaganda, or an incorrect proposition held by an uninformed subject. In his discussion of cynical reason and fantasy, especially pp. 24–31 of the 1989 Verso edition, he shifts attention from what people consciously believe to the way an illusion can structure their practical relation to social reality. The key formulation on p. 30 is that the illusion may reside in the activity itself: subjects can know how things stand and nevertheless continue to act as if the illusion were true (Žižek 1989, 28–31).

This distinction matters for the present argument. The paper does not claim that the public literally believes Lean verifies empirical reality, nor that all audiences mistake formal correctness for scientific truth. Rather, it asks whether the public and institutional practice of invoking machine verification can organize confidence as if a bounded technical check were a more comprehensive guarantee of scientific certainty.

The proposed concept, **the ideological fantasy of verification**, names this hypothesis. It concerns a public scene in which complex, distributed, and difficult-to-survey knowledge production appears to culminate in a simple, visible verdict: *verified*. The kernel's genuine reliability supplies the material support for this scene. The fantasy is not that the check is fake; it is that the check can appear to close a field of uncertainty much wider than the one it actually addresses.

The distinction can be stated precisely. Lean can check whether a proof term establishes a formal proposition under specified assumptions. It does not, by itself, establish that the formal proposition captures the intended informal theorem, that the theorem is empirically applicable, that the result is novel or important, or that the scientific community has understood and accepted its significance. The technical procedure reduces uncertainty at one level. The ideological fantasy arises if that reduction is represented or received as the closure of uncertainty as such.

## 3. The visible scene of verification

Why might formal verification be especially available for this symbolic role? Its public intelligibility is asymmetrical. The formal object may be highly technical, but the result of the checking procedure can be communicated in a compact form: the proof compiles; the kernel accepts it; independent checkers reproduce the result. A complex process becomes publicly legible through a short, apparently decisive credential.

This credential can perform several functions:

1. **Cognitive:** it supplies a proxy for assessing a proof that a non-specialist cannot inspect.
2. **Institutional:** it signals that a specified procedure of checking has been completed.
3. **Communicative:** it compresses a complicated chain of reasoning into a concise public claim.
4. **Affective:** it may offer reassurance in the face of specialized knowledge and uncertainty.
5. **Legitimating:** it may help institutions present a result as rigorous, objective, or settled.

These functions are not necessarily ideological in a pejorative sense. A formal certificate can be a warranted reason for confidence in the proposition it actually checks. The critical question is whether its public meaning remains bounded by that warrant.

The four Lean cases provide different tests. The Riemann case asks whether extensive machine checking can become a proxy for mathematical understanding. The Navier–Stokes case asks whether a formal proof can be made to stand for a separate natural-language proof whose correspondence to the formal declarations is disputed. The Erdős case asks whether transparent reporting of formalized benchmark scope and human statement validation can keep the credential bounded. The Liquid Tensor Experiment asks whether visible human collaboration resists the representation of the machine as an autonomous guarantor.

## 4. Fantasy and the relocation of uncertainty

A formal proof assistant does not abolish uncertainty. It relocates it. A successful kernel check can sharply reduce uncertainty about derivational validity. It leaves open questions about the choice of formal statement, the adequacy of definitions and assumptions, the correspondence with the intended mathematical problem, the significance of the result, and the institutional process through which it is accepted.

The ideological fantasy of verification may therefore be understood as a way of managing this relocation. Instead of confronting the continuing need for interpretation and judgment, a public narrative may treat the visible technical credential as if it were the final answer to the question of reliability.

This is not a causal claim that all audiences respond in this way. It is a hypothesis about the symbolic function that verification can perform. It must be tested against public announcements, media reports, expert commentary, and evidence of audience interpretation. Counterexamples matter: when a source clearly states the exact theorem checked, the human work involved, and the remaining questions, it may resist rather than reproduce the fantasy.

## 5. The explanatory gain

Hardwig explains why it is rational for the public to rely on experts and institutions. The concept of ideological fantasy asks a different question: why does a particular technical procedure become an especially compelling scene in which this reliance is represented as secure? The argument is not that rational dependence is secretly irrational. It is that a real and bounded form of assurance may acquire a symbolic function that helps a society relate to the uncertainty it cannot eliminate.

The provisional thesis of this section is therefore:

**Machine verification can become an ideological fantasy of scientific certainty when the public visibility of a bounded formal check stages the appearance that the uncertainty of specialized knowledge has been closed, even though the social and semantic conditions of scientific warrant remain in place.**

This claim is deliberately conditional. The case studies must establish when the symbolic enlargement occurs and when public communication preserves the limits of the technical result.
