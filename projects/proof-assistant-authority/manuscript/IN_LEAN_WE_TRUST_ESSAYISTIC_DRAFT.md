# In Lean We Trust? The Ideology of Machine-Checked Proofs in Science

**Project:** *In Lean We Trust?*  
**Version:** Essayistic philosophical draft  
**Evidence cut-off:** 27 November 2026  
**Genre:** Philosophical argument with comparative case analysis

## Abstract

What could be ideological about a correct proof? Lean’s kernel provides genuine and bounded warrant for a specified formal proposition under a specified environment. The question is how this local technical achievement can become a bearer of wider mathematical authority. Drawing on Slavoj Žižek’s critique of ideology, this article reconstructs a five-moment mechanism: the real reliability of the check; the distribution of epistemic labour across mathematicians, formalizers, software infrastructures, reviewers, and institutions; the condensation of this labour into the portable credential “Lean-verified”; the displacement through which authority generated by the network appears to reside autonomously in the credential; and fetishistic disavowal, through which symbolic investment can persist even when the limits of the check are known. The article develops this account as the **ideological fetishism of formal verification**. Four encounters with Lean—the Riemann hypothesis, Navier–Stokes, Erdős problems investigated with AlphaProof Nexus, and the Liquid Tensor Experiment—trace different configurations of certification, semantic mediation, and institutional recognition. Their comparison shows how a genuine technical warrant can acquire a symbolic surplus as it is condensed, circulated, and used to authorize mathematical claims. The article thus explains not only how the verification credential becomes an object of authority, but also how that authority can be sustained in practice despite knowledge of its limits.

**Keywords:** philosophy of science; philosophy of mathematical practice; social epistemology; political epistemology; Slavoj Žižek; ideology critique; formal verification; Lean; fetishism; mathematical proof

## 1. The Paradox of a Verified Proof

What is ideological about a correct proof?

Imagine that a mathematical proposition has been formalized, a proof term has been constructed, and Lean’s kernel accepts it. There is no need to look for a hidden failure in the machine. The proof assistant has performed the task for which it was designed. Under the specified formal environment, the term has the required type. The result is reproducible, inspectable, and, in principle, independently checkable. Why, then, should we speak of ideology? Would this not confuse a genuine mathematical achievement with a social illusion?

The question becomes difficult only when we abandon a familiar conception of ideology. If ideology means false consciousness, then the answer seems immediate: there is nothing ideological about a correct proof. One either has a valid derivation or one does not. Once the relevant facts are established, the ideological appearance should disappear.

But this is precisely the point at which Žižek’s critique becomes relevant. Ideology cannot always be identified by asking whether an explicit proposition is true or false. A statement may be factually correct and nevertheless participate in an ideological relation through the position it occupies and the practice it helps sustain (Žižek 1989; 1994). The question is not simply whether Lean’s verdict is correct. It is what this verdict is made to authorize.

Consider the difference between two statements:

> Lean’s kernel accepts a proof term for the formal proposition \(P^*\).

and

> The mathematical problem has been conclusively solved.

The first statement identifies a technical result. The second makes a claim about a mathematical achievement whose warrant may involve much more: the identity of \(P^*\) with the intended theorem, the adequacy of its definitions and assumptions, the relation between a formal artifact and a natural-language argument, and the status of the result within mathematical practice. The first does not automatically entail the second. Yet the public credential “Lean-verified” can travel between them, sometimes without the intervening relations being made visible.

The paradox is therefore not that a machine produces false certainty. It is that a machine may produce a genuine form of certainty, sharply bounded in its technical operation, while the credential associated with that achievement comes to signify something wider. The machine does not have to be unreliable for its authority to acquire an ideological function. On the contrary, its genuine reliability may be what makes the wider symbolic investment possible. A defective instrument could hardly serve as a convincing bearer of certainty.

This article asks how that transformation can occur. Its object is not Lean software in general, nor the psychological question of whether mathematicians trust machines. It is the public and institutional credential **“Lean-verified”**: the compact sign through which a complex formal and mathematical process becomes recognizable as a warrant for a result. The central hypothesis is that this credential may acquire an ideological function when the authority generated through a network of technical, semantic, and institutional relations appears to reside autonomously in the verification object itself.

The argument is not that formal verification is a deception, that mathematical truth is merely socially constructed, or that reliance on a proof assistant is irrational. A mathematician who relies on Lean for the task it actually performs may be entirely justified. Nor is every short description of a formal proof an instance of ideological distortion. The issue is more specific: under what conditions does a bounded technical credential become a symbolic guarantee of a wider mathematical claim, and what happens to the relations that connect the credential to that claim?

The question is worth asking because formal verification makes one part of mathematical warrant unusually visible. A proof may be extremely long, technically complex, and difficult for any individual mathematician to survey. Yet the result of kernel checking can be stated in a compact form. The complexity of the proof does not disappear; it becomes condensed in a portable credential. The word “verified” can then function as a point at which the otherwise distributed process of mathematical knowledge production seems to arrive at a final answer.

The following pages approach this problem through Žižek’s account of ideological fantasy and fetishism. The former helps explain how a local check can stage the appearance of closure; the latter helps explain how the authority generated by a network of relations can appear as an autonomous property of an object. Disavowal will enter only as a secondary possibility: it may help explain why symbolic investment persists even when limitations are acknowledged, but it is not necessary for every instance of fetishistic displacement.

Four encounters with Lean formalization will help clarify the argument. They are not four interchangeable examples, nor do they constitute a representative sample of proof-assistant practice. The Riemann hypothesis case concerns the relation between formal certification, institutional authorization, and foundational criticism. Navier–Stokes raises the problem of correspondence between a formal declaration and a separate natural-language proof. Erdős problems investigated with AlphaProof Nexus reveal the importance of human statement validation and artifact provenance. The Liquid Tensor Experiment provides a counter-case in which collective mathematical labour and semantic scrutiny are made unusually visible.

The guiding question throughout is simple, although its answer is not: when a proof is verified, what exactly has been verified—and what, beyond that, is the credential being asked to guarantee?

## 2. The Limits of Formal Certainty

The first task is to resist two opposite mistakes. The first is to treat formal verification as if it were merely a ceremonial endorsement of work already completed elsewhere. The second is to treat kernel acceptance as if it settled every question that can be asked about a mathematical result. Both mistakes obscure the distinctive epistemic value of formal verification.

Lean is a proof assistant based on dependent type theory. A proposition is represented as a formal type, and a proof is represented by a term inhabiting that type. The kernel checks whether the term has the required type relative to a specified formal environment, including definitions, axioms, imported libraries, and the trusted implementation. Schematically,

\[
\operatorname{KernelAccepts}(t : P^* \mid \Gamma)
\]

states that the proof term \(t\) is accepted as a proof of the formal proposition \(P^*\) under the environment \(\Gamma\).

This is a substantial achievement. It provides a highly reliable check of derivational validity relative to the formal system. It can exclude many classes of derivational error, support replay of proof artifacts, and allow independent implementations to check the exported proof. The fact that a proof was generated by an opaque AI system does not by itself remove this warrant. Duede and Davey (2025), for example, argue that a transparent proof checker can provide genuine mathematical knowledge even when the process that generated the proof is opaque. The check is not merely a performance staged for an audience. It does real epistemic work.

The question is what follows from that work.

Let \(P\) denote the intended mathematical claim and \(P^*\) the formal proposition submitted to Lean. Kernel acceptance establishes that a proof term proves \(P^*\) under \(\Gamma\). It does not, by that operation alone, establish the relation

\[
P \longleftrightarrow P^*.
\]

This relation concerns the adequacy of the formal specification: does the formal proposition express the mathematical claim that researchers intend to establish? It may be checked with great care. Definitions can be inspected, source statements compared, and the formalization scrutinized by mathematicians. In some settings, parts of the specification relation can themselves be formalized. The point is not that this relation is mysterious or impossible to establish. It is that checking a proof term and checking the adequacy of the proposition being proved are distinct epistemic tasks.

This distinction is now central to the philosophy of mathematical warrant. Vallverdú’s Locus Thesis argues that properties such as reliability, surveyability, transferability, and explanatory power should be assigned to components of a warrant architecture, rather than indiscriminately predicated of a proof as a whole (Vallverdú 2026). Kallel and El Louadi (2026) similarly distinguish derivational validity, representational fidelity, and epistemic significance. They argue that the abundance of machine-checked proofs does not remove the need for human adjudication; it changes where that adjudication is required.

The present article adopts this component-based account. It does not claim originality for the distinction between a formal proposition, a proof object, a checking procedure, a trusted base, and the intended mathematical claim. Nor does it claim that the gap between formal and informal mathematics is unbridgeable. The relevant point is more modest and more consequential: the warrant supplied by a kernel check is one component of a wider architecture, and the authority of the whole cannot simply be read off from the reliability of that component.

The same caution applies to the social organization of mathematics. A mathematician cannot personally reproduce every piece of software, inspect every library dependency, and independently reconstruct every proof used in a large formalization. Nor should this be expected. Scientific knowledge depends on a division of epistemic labour. Hardwig’s account of epistemic dependence explains why reliance on experts can be rational even when the person relying on them cannot personally reproduce their evidence (Hardwig 1985). De Millo, Lipton, and Perlis (1979) likewise emphasize that mathematical proof operates within social processes of criticism, communication, and acceptance.

There is nothing ideological merely in relying on Lean, trusting a competent formalizer, or accepting a well-documented institutional review. Such reliance can be rational. A journal may reasonably require a formal artifact; a mathematician may reasonably rely on independent kernel checking; a reader may reasonably trust experts who have compared a formal proposition with its intended statement.

This is where the problem becomes more precise. Social mediation is not ideology. Distributed labour is not fetishism. Institutional recognition is not automatically a distortion. The ideological question arises only when the credential associated with one component of the warrant architecture is made to appear as if it contains the authority of the whole. The problem is not that the machine checks something. It is that the result of this check may become the visible bearer of a certainty that depends on relations extending beyond the check itself.

## 3. Žižek and the Ideological Structure of Verification

### 3.1 Ideology beyond false consciousness

In “The Spectre of Ideology,” Žižek shifts the question of ideology away from the simple opposition between true and false beliefs (Žižek 1994). The ideological function of a proposition cannot be read directly from its truth value. A statement may be factually correct and nevertheless participate in an ideological relation through the position it occupies and the practice it helps sustain. What matters is not only whether a statement is true, but how its truth is mobilized within a social order.

“Lean checked this proof” may be entirely accurate. Its philosophical significance emerges when this true statement becomes the point through which a wider mathematical claim is authorized. The credential can remain attached to the formal proposition and the conditions under which it was checked; it can also travel as if it carried the authority of the entire mathematical result. The same technical fact can therefore occupy different positions within the production of knowledge.

Žižek’s account directs attention beyond explicit discourse. In “The Spectre of Ideology,” he distinguishes ideology as doctrine, its material externality in practices and institutions, and its more elusive spontaneous operation within social reality (Žižek 1994, 9–10). Applied to formal verification, this means that the credential must be studied across the settings in which it acquires force: the repository in which a proof is checked, the paper that reports it, the institution that recognizes it, and the practices through which mathematical credibility is distributed. Ideology is not simply a mistaken sentence about a machine. It concerns the relation between what is said, what is materially done, and what becomes authoritative.

### 3.2 Ideological fantasy: the scene in which uncertainty appears to end

In *The Sublime Object of Ideology*, fantasy is not merely a false image concealing an otherwise complete reality. It supplies the coordinates through which reality is experienced and sustains the appearance of coherence where a constitutive gap remains (Žižek 1989, 28–31). Fantasy organizes the scene in which a practice becomes intelligible and an incomplete relation can appear whole.

The relevant limit in formal verification is the non-identity between the formal object that is checked and the intended mathematical claim to which it is connected. Let \(P\) be the intended claim and \(P^*\) the formal proposition. The kernel may establish with extraordinary precision that a proof term \(t\) inhabits \(P^*\) under a specified environment \(\Gamma\). The relation between \(P\) and \(P^*\) is a distinct specification relation. It can be scrutinized, formalized in part, checked by experts, and made highly reliable. The point is that this work remains conceptually distinct from the kernel's acceptance of \(t : P^*\).

This is a question of non-totalizability, not an unbridgeable gulf. The specification relation may be rigorously established; the different components of warrant may be connected without becoming the same operation. The ideological possibility arises when the local verdict is staged as the point at which the wider work of specification, interpretation, and institutional assessment appears already complete.

The fantasy of formal verification is therefore a scene of closure. The certificate seems to settle not only whether \(t\) proves \(P^*\), but also whether \(P^*\) is the intended theorem, whether the assumptions are appropriate, whether the result has the significance attributed to it, and whether the mathematical dispute is over. The fantasy does not have to appear as the explicit belief that Lean can prove everything. Its operation is subtler: the visible completion of one task gives the appearance that the whole chain of warrant has reached completion.

The distinction can be written schematically:

\[
\operatorname{KernelAccepts}(t : P^* \mid \Gamma)
\]

is a technical result. But

\[
\operatorname{KernelAccepts}(t : P^* \mid \Gamma)
\Rightarrow
\text{the wider mathematical claim is fully settled}
\]

is not a consequence of kernel acceptance alone.

Why is this scene compelling? Formal verification makes one part of mathematical warrant unusually visible. A proof may depend on extensive libraries, complex formalization, and years of technical work, while the result of checking can be communicated in a compact and reproducible form. The word “verified” gives a heterogeneous process a single point of presentation. Fantasy names the way this local completion can appear as the completion of the whole.

### 3.3 Fetishism: when the relation appears as a property of the object

The more decisive theoretical move comes from Marx’s account of commodity fetishism. In *Capital*, Volume I, Chapter 1, Section 4, Marx analyzes how relations among producers assume the appearance of relations among things. Žižek’s discussion emphasizes how an effect generated by a network of relations can appear as an immediate property of an object (Žižek 1989, 19–20).

The analogy with formal verification is structural. Mathematical truth is not produced by collective labour in the same way that commodities are produced, and Lean is not a commodity in Marx’s technical economic sense. The relevant structure is the appearance of a relation-dependent authority as an intrinsic property of an object.

The authority of a Lean-verified result depends on a network: the problem is selected and formulated; definitions and assumptions are chosen; the intended theorem is represented formally; a proof object is constructed; libraries and software infrastructure are maintained; the kernel checks the proof term; experts examine the specification relation; institutions and mathematical communities assess the result’s status. These activities contribute different forms of warrant. Their combined authority is what allows the formal artifact to count as a proof of a particular mathematical claim.

The fetishistic possibility appears when the authority generated through this network seems to reside autonomously in the verification credential itself. The credential appears not merely to report that one component of the proof process has succeeded, but to contain the authority of the whole. The relation is inverted: the network makes the credential meaningful, yet the credential appears as the source that certifies the network.

Fantasy and fetishism therefore do different work. Fantasy supplies the scene in which the local check appears to close the wider question. Fetishism gives this appearance an objectal form: the closure seems to reside in the credential itself. The first concerns the staging of completeness; the second concerns the apparent location of the authority that completeness promises. They reinforce one another, but they are not interchangeable.

### 3.4 Disavowal and the persistence of symbolic investment

Žižek’s discussion of cynical ideology draws on Peter Sloterdijk’s critique of cynical reason (Sloterdijk 1987; Žižek 1989, ch. 1). The familiar formula that subjects know what they are doing and nevertheless continue to do it should not be presented as Žižek’s original coinage. What matters for the present argument is the distinction between cynical and fetishistic disavowal, developed in recent work by Kuldova (2019) and Black (2025, 118–119).

Cynical disavowal maintains a distance between what an actor knows and what the actor publicly or practically does, often by obscuring that distance. Fetishistic disavowal has a different structure. The subject can acknowledge both the objective state of affairs and the way in which the object nevertheless continues to appear and function. Knowledge does not simply dissolve the investment. The subject knows, and nonetheless continues.

In the context of formal verification, the relevant formula is not that mathematicians mistakenly believe Lean proves more than it does. A researcher may know perfectly well that the kernel checks a proof term for \(P^*\) under \(\Gamma\), and that the relation between \(P^*\) and the intended claim \(P\) is a distinct task. The question is whether this knowledge changes the practical and symbolic use of the credential. A researcher who relies on Lean for precisely the proposition it checks is engaged in rational epistemic dependence. The fetishistic possibility appears when the same credential continues to function as a broader sign of mathematical closure or authority, despite explicit knowledge that its technical warrant is narrower.

Disavowal therefore explains the persistence of symbolic investment, rather than the initial production of the credential. It is not necessary for every instance of objectal displacement. It becomes important when the credential continues to organize recognition and action after its limits have been acknowledged. The point is not necessarily conscious deception or hypocrisy. It is a split between what is known about the warrant and what the credential continues to make possible in practice.

The Lacanian idea that there is no complete Big Other and no “Other of the Other” provides a background to this argument (Lacan 2019, ch. XVI; Žižek 1989, Part II). No final symbolic authority guarantees the consistency of the entire order from outside it. The significance for formal verification is not that mathematical proof lacks foundations, but that the credential may be invested with the function of a final guarantee even when its technical scope is explicitly understood.

## 4. The Machine as a Fetish Object

How does the credential “Lean-verified” acquire this authority? The answer is not that a machine suddenly becomes an object of irrational admiration. Nor is it that a community simply mistakes a technical procedure for something it is not. The process is more interesting: a real technical achievement is produced through distributed labour, condensed into a portable sign, and then made to carry a symbolic authority that appears to belong to the sign itself.

The formation of the epistemic fetish can be reconstructed through five connected moments. They are analytically ordered rather than a rigid chronology: the first four explain how the credential acquires its objectal authority; the fifth explains how that authority can be reproduced even after its limits are known.

**First, the check has real technical efficacy.** Lean’s kernel does not merely symbolize reliability. It checks whether a proof term has the required type for a specified formal proposition under a specified environment. The result can be replayed, inspected, and, in appropriate settings, checked independently. This is the material basis of the credential’s credibility. The ideological function of formal verification does not arise because the machine fails to do its job. It becomes possible because the machine genuinely does something valuable. Its reliability is not an illusion that must be exposed; it is the real achievement around which a wider symbolic investment can gather.

**Second, the achievement is produced through distributed epistemic labour.** The formal result depends on more than the kernel. A mathematical problem must be selected and formulated; definitions and assumptions must be chosen; the intended claim must be represented as a formal proposition; a proof must be found and encoded; libraries and software tools must be developed and maintained; the trusted environment must be specified; the formal statement must be compared with the intended theorem; and the result must be interpreted and assessed by mathematicians, reviewers, and institutions. These activities are distributed across people, technical systems, and organizations. They contribute different kinds of epistemic work, and no single component performs the whole task.

This distribution is not a defect in mathematical knowledge. It is a condition of contemporary mathematical practice. It does, however, mean that the authority of a result is assembled across a network whose full operation may not be visible to any one participant. The result depends on relations of expertise, maintenance, interpretation, and institutional recognition. The technical check is one indispensable component within this larger arrangement.

**Third, scientific communication condenses this distributed process into a credential.** A complex mathematical achievement must travel. It appears in an abstract, a paper title, a repository description, a journal announcement, a university news item, a conference presentation, or a media report. These forms cannot reproduce the entire history of formalization and review. They need a compact way to identify what has been accomplished. “Lean-verified” performs this communicative function. It condenses theorem formulation, formal specification, proof construction, kernel checking, and layers of human scrutiny into a single portable status.

Condensation is necessary for communication. It is not yet fetishism. A concise statement can preserve the scope of the check, and a credential can accurately summarize a complex process. The decisive question is what happens when the condensed sign begins to circulate independently of the relations that initially fixed its meaning.

**Fourth, the credential acquires symbolic authority through objectal displacement.** As “Lean-verified” moves across contexts, the exact formal proposition, environment, assumptions, dependencies, and specification relation may become less visible, while the credential itself remains. The sign travels more easily than the network that supports it. It can then appear not merely as a report that one component of a warrant architecture has succeeded, but as an autonomous guarantee of the result as a whole.

Here the relation is inverted. The network makes the credential meaningful, yet the credential appears as the source that certifies the network. Authority generated through mathematical judgement, formalization, software infrastructure, checking, and institutional assessment seems to reside in the machine-certified object. The local warrant of the kernel is thereby given a symbolic surplus: “Lean-verified” can come to signify not only that a formal proposition has been checked, but that the wider mathematical claim is secure, settled, and institutionally authoritative. This is the specifically fetishistic moment. The object appears to possess, as its own property, an authority that in fact depends on the relations that produced and interpreted it.

**Fifth, fetishistic disavowal can reproduce this authority despite knowledge of its limits.** The credential need not be sustained by the naïve belief that Lean verifies everything. Participants may know exactly what the kernel checks. They may even explain the distinction between a formal proposition and the intended mathematical claim. Yet the credential can continue to function as the decisive sign through which the result is recognized, circulated, and treated as settled.

The difference between rational reliance and fetishistic disavowal is crucial. To know that Lean verifies \(P^*\) and to rely on it as evidence for \(P^*\) is entirely reasonable. The fetishistic structure appears when the credential retains a wider symbolic function after the boundary has been acknowledged: the subject knows that the check is local, but nonetheless continues to invest the credential with the appearance of a more comprehensive guarantee. The contradiction is not resolved by knowledge; it is sustained through practice. A qualification can coexist with the continued use of the credential as a marker of finality. The knowledge of the limit and the investment in the object occupy different registers.

This is not the same as cynical disavowal. Cynicism may conceal or manipulate the distance between knowledge and action. Fetishistic disavowal can openly acknowledge the limitation while continuing to act through the symbolic object that appears to overcome it. The credential becomes a means by which a community can proceed as if the wider chain of warrant were closed, even when its participants know that the kernel’s operation does not itself close every link in that chain. Its authority is reproduced through repeated acts of citation, endorsement, recognition, and communication.

The five moments can be summarized as follows:

\[
\begin{aligned}
&\text{Real technical reliability}\\
\longrightarrow\;&\text{Distributed epistemic labour}\\
\longrightarrow\;&\text{Credential condensation}\\
\longrightarrow\;&\text{Objectal displacement and symbolic authority}\\
\longrightarrow\;&\text{Fetishistic disavowal and reproduction}.
\end{aligned}
\]

This sequence is not a mechanical law, nor does every use of a formal verification system pass through each moment in the same way. It is a reconstruction of the mechanism by which a bounded technical achievement can become an object of wider epistemic authority. The first moment supplies the real warrant; the second distributes the labour that makes the result possible; the third gives that labour a portable form; the fourth turns the portable form into an apparently autonomous bearer of authority; and the fifth explains why this authority can persist even when its limits are known.

The fetish, then, is not simply the machine, and it is not a false belief about the machine. It is the credential through which the authority of a distributed epistemic network appears to be contained in a single object. The machine’s reliability remains real throughout this process. What changes is the symbolic work that its credential is made to perform.

## 5. Four Encounters with Formal Verification

The following cases are not a representative survey of Lean use, nor are they matched controls. They were selected because they expose different relations between a formal artifact and the mathematical claim associated with it. Each encounter is read alongside its primary statements, technical records, and relevant independent scrutiny. The purpose is not to adjudicate the underlying mathematics of every result. It is to ask what the Lean credential is made to warrant, what relations remain visible, and what the available evidence permits us to conclude.

### 5.1 The Riemann Hypothesis: certification and the limits of institutional closure

On 20 November 2026, Levent Alpöge released a note and a large Lean development concerning the Riemann hypothesis (Alpöge 2026). The Lean FRO and Mathlib Initiative issued a joint statement on 21 November expressing “full confidence” in the proof and stating that the community certified both the statement and the proof (Lean FRO and Mathlib Initiative 2026).

The formal statement itself matters. Mathlib’s definition of the Riemann hypothesis asserts that every zero of the formalized zeta function, excluding the trivial negative even zeros and the point \(s=1\), has real part \(1/2\) (Mathlib Documentation 2026). The exclusion of \(s=1\) is part of the formal definition because the classical zeta function has a pole there, while the formalized function assigns a value at that point. The formal object is therefore not simply a phrase copied from a textbook. It is a specific mathematical statement within a formal library.

The joint statement describes several forms of scrutiny: expert vetting of the Mathlib statement, successful compilation of the archive, comparison of the proved theorem with the Mathlib statement by two independent tools, and acceptance of the exported proof by Lean’s kernel and independent checkers. Andrew Sutherland’s verification report records 175,187 compiling modules in the original archive, kernel replay, independent checker runs, and statement comparisons (Sutherland 2026a). A shorter rewrite appeared on 25 November and has a separate verification record; it is a distinct artifact and should not be conflated with the original archive (Sutherland 2026b).

A substantial exposition also appeared on 26 November. The rh-lean-roadmap project provides a 497-page main text, 963 pages of longer proofs, a 122-page index, and a short roadmap. Its documentation states that several AI models generated the exposition from the Lean source and cross-checked it (rh-lean-roadmap 2026). This is a readable, AI-mediated mathematical account. It is not a conventional independently authored paper, nor does its existence prove that every mathematician has independently read and accepted every argument.

The case became more complicated when Parker Emmerson posted a non-peer-reviewed preprint on 24 November arguing that the certified derivation does not prove the Riemann hypothesis in several senses (Emmerson 2026). The preprint grants the existence of the Lean derivation and the reported checks, but challenges what follows from them. In particular, it treats the consistency of the formal rules as a further premise or “sponsor” and argues that this is stronger than the hypothesis. The official Lean FRO/Mathlib statement, for its part, says that the independent checkers have formal soundness proofs relative to set theory. The disagreement therefore concerns not whether the kernel accepted the proof term, but how the foundational conditions and formal statement should be interpreted.

This article does not adjudicate Emmerson’s mathematical or logical argument. Its status as a non-peer-reviewed preprint must remain explicit. It is evidence of a public foundational challenge, not proof that the Lean kernel failed or that the Riemann hypothesis is false. Conversely, the institutional statement is evidence of a substantial certification process, not a demonstration that every foundational and interpretive question has been settled.

What does the case show? It shows that several forms of authority coexist: kernel checking, independent checking, expert assessment of the formal statement, institutional certification, AI-mediated exposition, and foundational criticism. The official statement makes much of the human and institutional mediation visible. The public challenge, meanwhile, demonstrates that certification does not terminate every dispute about what has been proved.

The Riemann case is therefore not strong evidence of fetishistic displacement. The credential has considerable institutional force, but the process behind it is unusually visible. It is more useful as a case of layered and contested authorization: the machine check is real, the institutional certification is real, and the meaning of that certification remains open to philosophical and mathematical interpretation.

### 5.2 Navier–Stokes: a formal proof and the proof it is said to represent

On 8 September 2026, OpenAI announced a result concerning the Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem, presenting an analytical proof together with a Lean formalization (OpenAI 2026). The announcement concerns finite-time singularity formation for a three-dimensional incompressible fluid with smooth external forcing and finite energy, and claims to establish statements C and D of the official Millennium Prize formulation.

An independent analysis by Bastounis, Circelli, and Hansen (2026) examines the relation between the natural-language paper and the Lean formalization. Their central distinction is important: compiling a Lean proof of a formal statement is not the same as establishing that the formalization is a semantically faithful translation of the original mathematical text. Their audit identifies, among other things, a discrepancy in Lemma 8.6. The natural-language proof uses \(m+4\) input derivatives in an inverse estimate, whereas the corresponding Lean declaration requires \(m+5\). They also identify a difference in the pressure-flux estimate and proof route.

These discrepancies concern the relation between the written proof and the formal artifact. They do not establish a failure of Lean’s kernel. Nor do they prove that the underlying Navier–Stokes theorem is false. The kernel may correctly check a proof term for the formal proposition encoded in the repository while the correspondence between that proposition and the separate natural-language argument remains disputed.

This case makes the distinction between derivational validity and specification fidelity unusually concrete. The issue is not whether Lean has checked something. It is whether the thing checked is precisely the mathematical claim that the public account associates with it.

It is tempting to treat this as a clear example of ideological fetishism: a formal certificate appears beside a claim to have solved a Millennium Prize problem, while the relation between the formal declarations and the written proof is contested. But the evidence requires a more careful judgement. OpenAI’s announcement presents both a write-up and a formalization; it does not explicitly state that Lean alone verifies every step of the natural-language paper. The discrepancy is a serious reason to inspect the specification relation, but it is not by itself proof that the institution or its audience treated the credential as an autonomous guarantee.

Navier–Stokes is therefore the strongest candidate for scope extension among the four cases, but the full diagnosis remains unestablished. The case reveals the exact place where a machine-checked formal object and a broader mathematical claim can come apart. Whether the credential then functions as an autonomous bearer of authority requires additional evidence about its representation and legitimating effect.

### 5.3 Erdős problems and AlphaProof Nexus: the return of human validation

Tsoukalas and colleagues report that AlphaProof Nexus resolved nine of 353 open Erdős problems represented in the Formal Conjectures repository and produced 44 proofs among 492 OEIS conjectures attempted (Tsoukalas et al. 2026). These denominators describe the formalized benchmark and the attempted OEIS statements, not all open Erdős problems. The system combines language-model proof search with Lean checking. The *Science* paper also reports human validation of the correspondence between the nine solved Lean statements and the original conjectures.

This workflow is important because it makes the division of epistemic labour relatively explicit. The AI system searches for proof strategies; Lean checks formal proof terms; human experts assess whether the formal statements capture the intended conjectures. The machine is not simply presented as an autonomous mathematician whose output is self-authenticating.

A source-level audit nevertheless identifies several provenance and statement-link issues (Erdős Problems Research Library 2026). For problem #26, the top-level linked Lean target differs from the upper-density theorem described in the prose, although an auxiliary pointwise lemma in the same file is strong enough to establish the prose result. For #741(i), the linked file proves an exact-density counterexample, while the upper-density theorem exists separately in the Formal Conjectures repository. For #741(ii), the cited link points to a file for another problem. These findings concern the identity and provenance of particular artifacts. They do not show that Lean accepted an invalid proof, that all nine results are invalid, or that the relevant formal proofs do not exist elsewhere.

The case therefore reveals a distinction between a paper’s report of human statement validation and the traceability of each published artifact. The existence of human validation does not make every repository link self-authenticating. Conversely, a wrong or variant link does not refute the theorem. The precise question is whether the artifact cited as the formal proof is the artifact that establishes the claim described in the paper.

AlphaProof Nexus is not a clean counter-case, but neither is it a demonstrated instance of fetishism. It shows how a public credential can depend on the continuing work of statement comparison, version control, and provenance. It also shows why “Lean-verified” cannot be treated as a substitute for identifying which theorem, in which file, at which commit, was actually checked.

### 5.4 The Liquid Tensor Experiment: a counter-example to autonomous machine authority

The Liquid Tensor Experiment provides a different configuration. Completed in July 2022, the project formalized a major theorem concerning the vanishing of higher Ext groups for specified condensed real vector spaces (Mathlib Community 2022). Its public record identifies mathematical, formalization, technical, library, and institutional contributors. The formal result is presented as a collective undertaking, not as an autonomous achievement of the proof assistant.

More importantly, Adam Topaz’s subsequent discussion explicitly raises the semantic problem: how can mathematicians be confident that the definitions introduced in Lean are correct? A formal definition could be pathological—for example, it could make Ext identically zero. The project responded by creating an examples folder containing sanity checks designed to be readable by mathematicians with limited Lean experience, allowing them to inspect the definitions and gain reasonable confidence that the formal statement captured the intended mathematics (Topaz 2022). Peter Scholze’s account also emphasizes mathematical interaction and scrutiny (Scholze 2021).

This is not merely a project that happens to acknowledge human contributors. It makes the semantic validity of its definitions an explicit object of public scrutiny. The formal certificate remains embedded in a visible network of mathematical interpretation and technical work.

Liquid Tensor is therefore a genuine counter-case to any claim that formal verification is inherently fetishistic. It shows how the authority of a proof assistant can remain embedded in a broader, inspectable epistemic practice in which semantic scrutiny and collective mathematical labour are visible.

## 6. The Ideological Function of Mathematical Certainty

The four encounters reveal different points at which the authority of a formal check is produced, condensed, circulated, and contested. Their significance lies in the configurations they bring into view: technical certification, distributed mathematical labour, the compression of that labour into public credentials, semantic mediation, and institutional recognition. They show that the credential “Lean-verified” does not enter mathematical knowledge as a neutral label attached to an isolated technical event. It moves through the practices by which mathematical results become recognizable and authoritative.

Modern mathematics depends on forms of knowledge that no single participant can survey in their entirety. Proofs may rely on extensive libraries, complex software, specialized formalization, and distributed expert judgement. This division of epistemic labour is a condition of contemporary mathematical practice. It also creates a demand for portable warrants: ways of allowing results to travel beyond the immediate group that constructed and checked them. A credential such as “Lean-verified” answers this demand. It condenses a complex achievement into a form that researchers, institutions, and wider audiences can recognize.

The same portability gives the credential its ambivalent force. It can preserve the result of a precise local check while allowing that result to circulate beyond the proposition, environment, and specification relation that initially fixed its meaning. The credential can thus perform two functions at once: it reports that a formal proof has been checked, and it can become a sign through which the wider mathematical claim is recognized as settled. The first function belongs to the kernel. The second emerges through the social and symbolic life of the credential.

The Riemann hypothesis case shows how formal checking, independent verification, expert assessment of the formal statement, institutional certification, and AI-mediated exposition converge in a public event. The Lean FRO/Mathlib statement expresses full confidence in the proof, while the later Emmerson preprint contests the foundational interpretation of what the certification establishes. The case brings into view a layered structure of authorization: technical checking, expert scrutiny, institutional recognition, and public contestation all contribute to the meaning of the result.

Navier–Stokes brings the relation between formal validity and specification fidelity into sharper focus. The discrepancy between the natural-language proof and the Lean declarations raises a precise question about the relation between the formal object and the mathematical claim it is said to represent. A proof term may be correctly checked for the formal statement while the correspondence between that statement and the written argument remains disputed. The case therefore exposes a point at which the credential can acquire significance beyond the formal proposition: the public meaning of the verification depends on how the formal and natural-language proofs are connected.

The Erdős / AlphaProof Nexus case reveals another configuration. The published account reports human validation of the correspondence between formal statements and original conjectures. The source-level provenance issues show how the identity of a cited formal artifact remains part of the warrant for the published result. The case brings the human and computational elements of proof production into the same frame: proof search, formal checking, statement validation, and artifact traceability jointly shape the authority of the result.

Liquid Tensor provides a contrasting configuration. The project made collective mathematical labour and the correctness of formal definitions explicit objects of scrutiny. Topaz’s sanity checks were designed to make the definitions inspectable by mathematicians who were not necessarily experienced Lean users. Here, the authority of the formal result is presented through a visible process of mathematical interpretation and technical work. The credential remains connected to the practices that make it meaningful.

The cases can now be read through the five-moment mechanism. The first two moments—the real efficacy of the check and the distribution of epistemic labour—are visible across all four projects, though in different configurations. The Riemann case makes the institutional condensation of multiple checks and expert judgements especially visible. Navier–Stokes exposes the point at which a formal credential can travel alongside a broader mathematical claim whose relation to the formal declaration requires scrutiny. Erdős shows that human statement validation and artifact provenance remain part of the credential’s warrant. Liquid Tensor demonstrates a practice in which the semantic and collective conditions of formalization are made visible rather than absorbed into the machine’s verdict.

The fifth moment changes how we understand the persistence of authority. The question is not simply whether participants know that Lean has limits. In sophisticated mathematical practice, such knowledge may be explicit. The question is what the credential continues to do after those limits have been stated. Does it remain a report of a bounded check, or does it continue to function as a general sign of mathematical completion? Fetishistic disavowal names the possibility that these two registers coexist: the scope of the check is known, while the credential retains a symbolic investment that exceeds that scope. The institutional life of a qualification therefore matters as much as its textual presence. A limitation may be stated in a technical document while the credential continues to circulate as a broader marker of authority.

The theoretical contribution lies in distinguishing the **warrant of a check** from the **authority of a credential**. The first concerns what the formal system establishes. The second concerns how that result is recognized, circulated, and made to count. A credential can report a real technical achievement while also becoming the bearer of a wider symbolic authority. The ideological question is how that surplus authority is produced: how the relations that sustain the check are condensed into an object that appears to possess its authority in itself, and how this appearance can be reproduced even when its limits are known.

This argument enters into conversation with recent work on mathematical warrant and AI-assisted proof. Vallverdú’s Locus Thesis provides a component-based account of mathematical warrant (Vallverdú 2026). Kallel and El Louadi (2026) distinguish derivational validity, representational fidelity, and epistemic significance, while examining the relation between abundant checking and human adjudication. López-Corona’s account of “studio mathematics” analyzes human-machine assemblages, provenance, agency, and responsibility in AI-assisted proof production (López-Corona 2026). These works clarify how mathematical warrant is distributed across technical and human practices. The present argument adds a Žižekian question about the form in which the authority generated through those practices appears and circulates.

Erfani (2026) examines ideological functions of AI systems, while Hourigan (2009) applies Žižek to technological fetishization and knowledge production. Omodeo (2019) develops a political epistemology of ideology in science studies. Against this background, the present article focuses on a specific object: the credential “Lean-verified.” Its contribution is to explain how the genuine warrant of formal checking can become a symbolic bearer of wider mathematical authority, and how fetishistic disavowal can sustain that authority even after its technical limits are understood. The phrase “epistemic fetishism” has also been used in a different analytic-epistemological sense by Ahlstrom-Vij (2021); the present article therefore uses the full expression “the ideological fetishism of formal verification” and does not claim to have coined the bare term.

The ideological function of mathematical certainty is not that certainty is false. Formal verification can provide a real and powerful form of certainty. Its significance lies in the way this certainty travels. A local technical guarantee becomes socially consequential when it is recognized, circulated, and used as a credential for a mathematical result. The same process that makes the credential useful also creates the possibility that its authority will exceed the warrant it records. The machine’s reliability supplies the material support for this symbolic authority; the credential gives it a portable form; and fetishistic disavowal can preserve its force after the boundary of the check is known.

## 7. Conclusion

How does the epistemic fetishism of formal verification arise? It does not begin with a false belief about an unreliable machine. It begins with a real technical achievement. Lean’s kernel checks proof terms against formal propositions under specified environments, and this genuine reliability gives the credential “Lean-verified” its initial force.

The authority of the credential is then assembled through distributed epistemic labour. Mathematical formulation, formal specification, proof construction, library and software maintenance, kernel checking, semantic comparison, expert assessment, and institutional recognition contribute different forms of warrant. Scientific communication condenses this distributed process into a portable sign. As the credential circulates, the relations that gave it meaning can recede while the sign remains. The authority of the network then appears to reside autonomously in the machine-certified object. This is the fetishistic displacement at the centre of the argument.

The process does not end when participants become aware of the credential’s limits. Fetishistic disavowal explains how symbolic investment can persist alongside such knowledge. One may know that Lean checks a formal proposition rather than every relation through which that proposition becomes a mathematical claim, and nonetheless continue to invest in “Lean-verified” as a sign of wider certainty. This is not the same as rationally relying on Lean for the proposition it actually checks, nor is it necessarily cynical deception. It is the persistence of an objectal guarantee in practice despite knowledge of the boundary that qualifies it.

The four encounters show different configurations of this process. Riemann brings technical checking, expert assessment, institutional certification, and foundational contestation into one field. Navier–Stokes exposes the significance of the relation between a checked formal declaration and the natural-language proof it is associated with. Erdős foregrounds human statement validation and artifact provenance. Liquid Tensor shows how semantic scrutiny and collective mathematical labour can remain visible within a formalization project. Together, these cases illuminate how formal verification becomes part of the practices through which mathematical results acquire public and institutional authority.

The article’s central contribution is the concept of the **ideological fetishism of formal verification**: the transformation through which the socially and semantically mediated authority of a machine-checked result appears to reside autonomously in the credential “Lean-verified.” The five-moment account explains how this transformation is formed and reproduced: real reliability, distributed epistemic labour, credential condensation, objectal displacement, and fetishistic disavowal. The machine’s reliability is not what ideology must explain away. It is precisely what ideology must explain: how a real technical guarantee becomes the material support for a symbolic authority that exceeds the operation which produced it.

The machine verifies a proposition. The philosophical question is what, beyond that proposition, we ask the credential to guarantee.
