# Revised theoretical framework: the ideological fetishism of formal verification

**Project:** *In Lean We Trust? The Ideology of Machine-Checked Proofs in Science*  
**Date:** 27 November 2026  
**Status:** controlling conceptual framework. The operational model, diagnostic thresholds, and four-case tests are specified in THEORETICAL_MODEL.md and THEORY_CASE_TESTS.md. This document supersedes earlier formulations that placed the Big Other or the subject-supposed-to-know on an equal footing with Žižek's critique of ideology.

## 1. The paper's exact object

The paper does not ask simply why people trust Lean, whether Lean is reliable, or whether the public misunderstands formal proof. It asks:

**How does a technical procedure of formal verification, whose epistemic scope is determinate, acquire and exercise an ideological function within scientific knowledge production and public communication?**

The core phenomenon is a possible gap between:
- what Lean actually checks: whether a formal proof term establishes a specified formal proposition under specified definitions, axioms, and a trusted environment;
- what the formal check may be made to signify in scientific discourse: objectivity, completeness, finality, or the settlement of a broader scientific claim.

The paper investigates how a real, bounded technical reliability can become a symbolic guarantee of wider scientific certainty. A formally correct proof can coexist with an ideological representation of its authority. The ideological analysis does not imply that the proof is false or that justified reliance on it is irrational.

## 2. The central hypothesis

**The Ideological Fetishism of Formal Verification**

The epistemic authority of formal verification may be ideologically sustained when the genuine reliability of local machine checking is represented as an autonomous guarantee of broader scientific certainty, while the human, conceptual, semantic, and institutional conditions that make the verification meaningful recede from view.

This is the paper's proposed synthesis, not a pre-existing term in Žižek. Its elements must be demonstrated separately rather than inferred from the mere presence of Lean.

## 3. Žižek's three ideological mechanisms

### 3.1 Ideological fantasy

Fantasy is not a false belief or wishful thinking. In Žižek's account it structures how social reality is experienced and sustained. The hypothesis is that formal verification can stage a scene in which uncertainty appears to culminate in a simple verdict: verified.

The fantasy does not require anyone to believe that Lean proves empirical reality. It may operate when a bounded check is practically or rhetorically allowed to stand for a much broader guarantee. The key empirical question is whether the local certainty of the kernel is made to symbolize closure of uncertainty at other levels: semantic correspondence, empirical adequacy, novelty, expert acceptance, or public significance.

### 3.2 Fetishism and reification

Marx's commodity fetishism concerns the historically specific commodity form, in which relations among producers assume the appearance of relations among things. Žižek's reading emphasizes how an effect generated by a network of relations can appear as an immediate property of one element.

Our proposed extension, **epistemic fetishism of formal verification**, concerns a similar representational displacement:

**collective epistemic labour and institutional relations → apparent autonomous authority of the machine.**

The relevant network includes theorem selection, mathematical formulation, formalization, definitions and libraries, Lean development and maintenance, kernel checking, semantic validation, expert scrutiny, institutional recognition, and public communication.

Fetishism is not established by saying “Lean verified the theorem.” That may be accurate shorthand. It requires evidence that the social relations producing and interpreting the verification are displaced, while the machine appears to possess an autonomous authority. The commodity analogy is structural, not an identity: Lean is not a commodity in Marx's technical sense.

### 3.3 Disavowal: cynical and fetishistic forms

Disavowal is relevant because the paper is interested in cases where explicit knowledge of a limitation may coexist with practices that continue to invest verification with a broader authority. But the two forms must not be conflated.

Jack Black (2025), “Examining the Mechanism of Disavowal and Its Two Forms: Cynical Disavowal and Fetishistic Disavowal,” *Theory & Psychology* 35(1), 117–135, DOI 10.1177/09593543241308199, argues that cynical and fetishistic disavowal differ. Cynical disavowal involves maintaining a manipulative distance and obscuring the gap between belief and action; fetishistic disavowal can acknowledge the objective state of affairs while sustaining the fetishistic attachment that manages the Other's lack.

**Cynical disavowal in this project** is a strict empirical hypothesis: an actor or institution explicitly acknowledges that Lean's check has a limited scope, yet continues to communicate or act as if the check guarantees a wider claim. A single caveat next to an ambitious headline is not enough. We need evidence linking the acknowledged limit and the continued overextended practice to the same relevant actor or institutional communication chain.

**Fetishistic disavowal** may be more pertinent to the paper's central thesis: the limitation can be known and even stated, while the technical object continues to function as the thing through which a broader guarantee is sustained. The formula is not simply “they do not know”; rather, the symbolic investment can persist alongside knowledge of the limitation.

Black's distinction is a refinement of the coding framework, not a replacement for Žižek's broader theory of ideology. The paper should not diagnose unconscious motives from a press release or a headline.

## 4. The role of Lacan: supporting concepts, not a competing framework

Žižek remains the central theoretical framework. Lacan supplies conceptual resources that clarify how symbolic authority is attributed and why no final guarantee is available.

- **Subject-supposed-to-know (Seminar XI, Chapter XVIII):** a position constituted in transference. It can illuminate the attribution of a knowing position, but it is not a general synonym for public trust and must not be used to diagnose audiences or software.
- **Big Other / no Other of the Other (Seminar VI, 8 April 1959, Chapter XVI):** the symbolic locus of norms and authorization has no final external signifier that guarantees its consistency. Lean is not the Big Other; it may be positioned as a technical credential within a symbolic order that seeks a guarantee.

These Lacanian concepts are supporting explanations within the Žižekian argument. They are not independent co-equal theories and do not displace the analysis of ideological fantasy, fetishism, and disavowal.

## 5. How Hardwig fits

Hardwig is retained as a normative baseline, not as the paper's central critical theory. His account explains why reliance on experts and epistemic communities can be rational even when a person cannot personally reproduce the relevant evidence.

The distinction is:
- **Hardwig:** when is reliance on a scientific expert or institution epistemically warranted?
- **Žižek:** how does a form of warrant acquire a symbolic and ideological function in social practice, and how can a local guarantee be invested with the role of a broader guarantee?

An instance of justified reliance is not automatically ideological fetishism. The Žižekian analysis must identify an additional representational or practical mechanism beyond ordinary expert dependence.

## 6. The central mechanism: conditions and ideological operations

The operational model is not a deterministic six-stage sequence. It distinguishes the material conditions that make formal verification meaningful from the ideological operations that may enlarge its authority.

**Conditions of possibility**
- Lean supplies a genuine, bounded technical warrant.
- The result depends on collective mathematical, formal, technical, semantic, and institutional mediation.
- No single technical or institutional credential provides a final guarantee of every level of scientific knowledge.
- Experts and institutions connect the formal artifact to the intended mathematical claim and its significance.

**Three ideological operations**
1. **Fantasy:** the local check can stage a scene in which wider scientific uncertainty appears to reach closure.
2. **Fetishism:** the network that makes the check meaningful can appear as an autonomous property of the machine.
3. **Disavowal:** if the relevant limits are acknowledged, the broader symbolic investment may nevertheless persist. This is conditional and requires additional evidence.

Fantasy and fetishism are mutually reinforcing analytical dimensions, not consecutive stages. Disavowal is not necessary for every instance of fetishism.

**Three empirical planes**, following Žižek's “The Spectre of Ideology,” organize the case evidence:
- explicit discourse and claims;
- material-institutional procedures and apparatuses;
- repeated spontaneous-practical reliance.

A strong diagnosis requires an identified local technical warrant, a demonstrable scope enlargement, displacement of collective mediation into apparent machine autonomy, and repeated or institutionally consequential practice. Ordinary shorthand, context-preserving qualifications, visible human work, and semantic audits count as counter-evidence.

See THEORETICAL_MODEL.md for the complete derivation, evidence threshold, and falsification criteria.

## 7. Empirical focus and case logic

The four Lean cases remain fixed:

| Case | What it tests |
|---|---|
| Riemann / Claude + Lean | How formal checking, expert-community certification, and AI-mediated exposition combine as a public scene of mathematical certainty |
| Navier–Stokes / OpenAI + Lean | Whether kernel-checked declarations are made to stand for a disputed correspondence with a separate natural-language proof |
| Erdős / AlphaProof Nexus | How publication, expert validation, and exact proof-artifact provenance interact; distinguish a mismatched top-level target from auxiliary lemmas that establish the prose result |
| Liquid Tensor Experiment | Whether explicit definition sanity checks and visible collective labour resist the autonomous-machine representation |

Each case is examined through the same evidence chain: (a) exact formal proposition and proof artifact; (b) actual human and institutional labour; (c) official author/institutional description; (d) third-party and media representation; (e) available evidence of audience uptake. Public texts alone establish representations and invitations to trust, not what society as a whole believes.

## 8. Prior research and the originality boundary

- **Flisfeder (2023), “There is no metalanguage; or, Truth has the structure of a fiction,”** provides a contemporary systematic entry into Žižek's Hegelian-Lacanian ideology critique and the relation between post-ideology and post-truth. It is theoretical background, not an application to Lean proof assistants.
- **Hourigan (2009), *The Phantasmatic Subject of Technology: Slavoj Žižek, Techne, and the Abyss*,** directly applies Žižek to technology, technological fetishization, and knowledge production. It rules out any claim that ours is the first Žižekian critique of technology. Our distinction is the specific epistemic and institutional role of machine-checked proof in science.
- **Black (2025)** supplies a recent distinction between cynical and fetishistic disavowal. It sharpens the empirical coding of cases where acknowledged limitations coexist with continued symbolic investment.
- **Hauswald (2025), “Artificial Epistemic Authorities,”** analyzes whether AI systems can count as epistemic authorities and how non-testimonial deference to AI, scientific communities, and institutions should be understood. It is a close social-epistemological comparator, but does not supply the Žižekian account of formal verification as an ideological guarantee.
- **Hardwig (1985), De Millo, Lipton & Perlis (1979), and Kallel & El Louadi (2026)** remain essential prior work on expert dependence, social proof acceptance, and the distinction between derivational validity, representational fidelity, and epistemic significance.

**Defensible originality claim:** this paper develops a Žižekian account of how the bounded formal reliability of Lean verification can be invested with broader scientific authority through ideological fantasy, fetishistic displacement of collective epistemic labour, and forms of disavowal. It focuses specifically on the formalized proof as an epistemic object in contemporary scientific knowledge production and public communication.

## 9. What the paper will not claim

1. That Lean is unreliable or that formal verification is merely ideological.
2. That all trust in Lean or all reliance on experts is irrational.
3. That every use of the phrase “Lean-verified” is a case of fetishism.
4. That an actor is cynical merely because a public statement contains both a caveat and a strong claim.
5. That Lean is the Big Other or the subject-supposed-to-know.
6. That public-text analysis reveals unconscious motives or population-level beliefs.
7. That this is the first application of Žižek to technology or AI.

## 10. Central thesis for the paper

**Formal verification can become an ideological practice in scientific knowledge production when the genuine reliability of a bounded machine check is made to function as a symbolic guarantee of broader scientific certainty. A Žižekian analysis explains how this guarantee is sustained through fantasy, how collective epistemic relations can be displaced into the apparent autonomy of the machine, and how disavowal can preserve this symbolic investment even when its limits are known.**
