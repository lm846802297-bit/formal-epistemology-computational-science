# Primary-text derivation of the Žižekian model

**Project:** *In Lean We Trust?*  
**Date:** 27 November 2026  
**Status:** source map for the controlling model in THEORETICAL_MODEL.md. References are to the first English editions identified below. The revised manuscript paraphrases; it does not reproduce extended passages.

## 1. Primary-text strategy

The minimum theoretical basis is two Žižek texts:
1. *The Sublime Object of Ideology* (1989), especially Chapter 1, “How Did Marx Invent the Symptom?”
2. “The Spectre of Ideology” (1994), in *Mapping Ideology*, pp. 1–33.

Marx and Lacan are theoretical sources internal to Žižek’s framework. Hardwig remains a normative comparator for justified epistemic dependence. Sloterdijk is the source of the critique of cynical reason on which Žižek draws. None of these authors is treated as a parallel core theory.

## 2. Žižek, *The Sublime Object of Ideology* (1989)

### 2.1 Commodity fetishism

**Primary locator:** Chapter 1, “How Did Marx Invent the Symptom?”, section “Commodity Fetishism”; first English edition, especially pp. 19–20.

Žižek emphasizes that commodity fetishism is not simply the replacement of people by things. Its crucial structure is a misrecognition in which an effect of a structured network of relations appears as an immediate property of one element within that network.

**Application:** the authority of a Lean-verified result depends on formal specification, proof construction, libraries, kernel and toolchain, semantic comparison, expert scrutiny, and institutional recognition. The fetishistic hypothesis is that this network-generated authority appears to reside autonomously in the credential “Lean-verified.”

**Limit:** this is a structural analogy, not an identity with the commodity form. The article does not claim that Lean is a commodity in Marx’s technical economic sense or that social labour creates mathematical truth.

### 2.2 Cynical ideology and fetishistic disavowal

**Primary locators:** Chapter 1, “How Did Marx Invent the Symptom?”; first English edition, pp. 12–13 and 24–32.

Žižek introduces the formula “I know very well, but still” in his discussion of fetishistic disavowal and money (pp. 12–13). The key point is that conscious knowledge of money’s material character does not dissolve the practical investment through which it functions as the embodiment of wealth.

Žižek later develops the argument through commodity fetishism. Individuals know that money is an expression of social relations, yet in their actual social activity they act as if it were the immediate embodiment of wealth (pp. 28–30). He calls them “fetishists in practice, not in theory.” He then describes belief as externalized in effective procedures and objects: the social object can appear to believe in place of the subjects who use it (pp. 31–32). This is the most direct primary-text basis for the article’s fifth moment.

This fetishistic structure must be distinguished from cynical reason. In the section “Cynicism as a Form of Ideology” (approximately pp. 24–27), Žižek draws on Sloterdijk’s *Critique of Cynical Reason* (1987) to discuss subjects who know the distance between ideological appearance and social reality yet continue to insist on the mask. Cynical reason and fetishistic disavowal are related but not interchangeable. Kuldova (2019) and Black (2025) offer useful later distinctions, but Žižek’s own account of fetishistic practice and externalized belief supplies the central theoretical derivation.

**Application:** in formal verification, public acknowledgement of human problem selection, semantic validation, and mathematical exposition does not necessarily dissolve the public attribution of the achievement to an autonomous machine. The relevant object is the credential and the communication practice through which the machine remains the apparent bearer of the achievement.


### 2.3 Ideological fantasy

**Primary locator:** Chapter 1, section “Ideological Fantasy”; first English edition, approximately pp. 28–31.

Fantasy is not merely a false image concealing a complete reality. It supplies coordinates through which social reality is experienced and can sustain an appearance of consistency where a constitutive gap remains. Žižek’s later discussion of fantasy as the support of reality clarifies that fantasy is operative in social practice, not simply in private imagination.

**Application:** a local kernel verdict may be staged as the point at which the wider specification and interpretive work appears already settled. The fantasy does not eliminate the gap between the formal proposition and intended claim; it makes the work of bridging that gap appear complete.

**Limit:** not every concise technical statement or use of “verified” performs this function. A fantasy diagnosis requires evidence of the way the representation organizes a practice or institutional relation.

## 3. Žižek, “The Spectre of Ideology” (1994)

Edition: Slavoj Žižek, “The Spectre of Ideology,” in *Mapping Ideology*, ed. Slavoj Žižek (London: Verso, 1994), pp. 1–33.

### 3.1 Ideology is not reducible to falsity

**Primary locator:** “Critique of Ideology, today?”, especially p. 8 in the first English edition.

Žižek argues that a proposition may be factually accurate while its ideological function depends on the subjective position and social relation in which it is embedded.

**Application:** “Lean checked this proof” may be true. The analysis asks what that statement is made to authorize and whether the credential is made to stand for a broader claim than the formal warrant supports.

### 3.2 Three planes of ideology

**Primary locator:** “Critique of Ideology, today?”, approximately pp. 9–10.

Žižek distinguishes:
1. ideology as doctrine or explicit ideas;
2. ideology in its material externality, practices, rituals, and institutions;
3. the more elusive spontaneous ideology operating at the heart of social reality.

These are empirical planes, not three additional theoretical mechanisms. The present study translates them into:
- discursive representations;
- material-institutional procedures;
- repeated spontaneous-practical reliance.

The third plane cannot be inferred from a single public statement.

## 4. Marx and Lacan as sources within Žižek

### 4.1 Marx

Karl Marx, *Capital*, vol. I, ch. 1, §4, “The Fetishism of Commodities and the Secret Thereof.”

Marx’s account supplies the social-form structure of fetishism. Žižek’s reading emphasizes how a network-generated effect appears as a property of an object. The article borrows this structure without claiming identity between mathematical labour and capitalist commodity production.

### 4.2 Lacan

Jacques Lacan, *Desire and Its Interpretation: The Seminar of Jacques Lacan, Book VI*, ch. XVI, “There Is No Other of the Other” (English translation, Polity, 2019).

The absence of a final symbolic guarantor is a background premise. It does not imply that mathematical knowledge is impossible. Lean is not the Big Other; the question is whether the credential is made to function as if it guaranteed every level of mathematical authority.

## 5. Disavowal: attribution and operational use

The article distinguishes three levels:

1. **Žižek’s primary structure:** fetishistic illusion is embodied in social activity even where actors know the social relations behind the fetish (Žižek 1989, 12–13, 28–32).
2. **The later conceptual distinction:** Kuldova (2019) and Black (2025) clarify the difference between cynical and fetishistic disavowal. This distinction is used to avoid conflating strategic concealment with the persistence of practical investment.
3. **The present application:** the public communication chain acknowledges human and semantic mediation while continuing to attribute the mathematical achievement to an apparently autonomous machine.

The fifth moment is not inferred from a participant’s private psychology. It is identified in public practices of attribution: the same research and institutional communication chain names human problem selection, statement validation, and mathematical exposition, yet retains the machine as the autonomous subject of “resolution.” The analysis therefore concerns the public organization of belief and authority, not an unobservable mental state.


## 6. Derivation summary

1. **Beyond false consciousness:** ideological function cannot be read directly from factual truth or falsity.
2. **Fantasy:** a bounded check may stage the appearance that a wider specification and interpretive gap has been closed.
3. **Fetishism:** authority generated by a network of technical, semantic, and institutional relations may appear to reside autonomously in the credential.
4. **Fetishistic disavowal:** the broader investment may persist despite acknowledged limits. This is the reproductive moment of the mechanism and requires evidence linking boundary knowledge to continued broader use.
5. **Empirical planes:** explicit discourse, material-institutional practice, and spontaneous-practical reliance must be examined separately.

The operational diagnostic is S — scope extension, O — objectal displacement, and E — social efficacy. Technical warrant is the baseline, not a diagnostic indicator. See THEORETICAL_MODEL.md.
