# Primary-text reading and theoretical argument plan

**Project:** *In Lean We Trust? The Ideology of Machine-Checked Proofs in Science*  
**Date:** 27 November 2026  
**Status:** first source-based reading map and argument plan. Page references must be checked against the exact edition used in the manuscript.

## 1. Research decision

The paper's originality is the Žižekian analysis itself: applying Žižek's theory of ideology to explain how Lean verification can become a symbolic guarantee of scientific certainty in contemporary society. Hardwig, De Millo, Kallel and public-trust research are interlocutors and prior literature, not co-equal theoretical centres.

The three theoretical mechanisms are:
1. **Ideological fantasy of verification:** how a bounded technical check may stage the closure of scientific uncertainty.
2. **Epistemic fetishism of formal verification:** how collective epistemic labour may appear as an autonomous property of the machine. This is our proposed extension, not Žižek's own term.
3. **The Big Other and the missing guarantee:** how Lean may be positioned as a credential within a symbolic order that seeks a final guarantor, although no complete Big Other exists. Lean itself is not the Big Other.

## 2. Žižek: *The Sublime Object of Ideology* (1989)

Primary edition: Slavoj Žižek, *The Sublime Object of Ideology* (London: Verso, 1989; later editions differ in pagination). Publisher record: https://www.versobooks.com/products/1284-the-sublime-object-of-ideology

### 2.1 Chapter 1: “How Did Marx Invent the Symptom?”

Read the chapter as a theory of form, not as a simple claim that an ideology hides a false content. Žižek's Marx–Freud homology directs attention to why a social content assumes a particular form. In commodity analysis, the theoretical task is not exhausted by discovering labour behind value; one must explain why social labour takes the commodity form.

**Application:** do not ask only what collective labour lies behind a Lean proof. Ask why this distributed labour and its authority can appear in the form of an autonomous technical object that “verifies.” The object-form and the social conditions of its authority are the topic.

### 2.2 Chapter 1, around pp. 28–30 in the 1989 English edition

Žižek distinguishes an illusion located in what people know from an illusion that structures their actual social activity. The passage around p. 30 describes the overlooked illusion structuring the subject's effective relation to reality as ideological fantasy. The well-known formula “they know very well how things really are, but still they are doing it as if they did not know” is part of Žižek's discussion of cynical ideology and is indebted to Peter Sloterdijk's account of cynical reason; do not present the formula as an unqualified original coinage by Žižek.

**Application:** the relevant hypothesis is not that laypeople falsely believe Lean proves empirical reality. It is that the public and institutional practice of invoking “Lean-verified” may organize trust as if a technical credential supplied a more comprehensive guarantee than it actually does. Evidence must show this practical structuring, not merely a mistaken belief.

### 2.3 Later chapters: fantasy, the sublime object, and the Big Other

Use the later discussion of fantasy, the constitution of the sublime object, and the lack in the symbolic order to clarify how a particular object can appear to support a wider social consistency. Do not collapse the sublime object, objet petit a, the Big Other, and the subject-supposed-to-know into one concept.

## 3. Žižek: “The Spectre of Ideology” in *Mapping Ideology* (1994)

Primary source: Slavoj Žižek, “The Spectre of Ideology,” in *Mapping Ideology*, ed. Slavoj Žižek (London: Verso, 1994), pp. 1–33 in the first English edition. Full text locator: https://analepsis.org/wp-content/uploads/2011/08/415_mapping-ideology-slavoj-zizek.pdf

Read especially the discussion of ideology beyond explicit doctrine: the apparently non-ideological practices and presuppositions that reproduce a social order. This helps avoid reducing the present project to a media-language study. The object is also the institutional practice of presenting, certifying, circulating, and relying on formal verification.

**Application:** examine the whole circuit of Lean theorem development, formal checking, expert validation, institutional announcement, and public interpretation. An ideological effect, if present, may reside in the practical organization of authority, not in a single false sentence.

## 4. Marx: *Capital*, Volume I, Chapter 1, Section 4

Primary source: Karl Marx, *Capital*, vol. I, ch. 1, §4, “The Fetishism of Commodities and the Secret Thereof.” Text: https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1867-c1/ch01.htm

Marx's central formulation is that a definite social relation between people assumes the fantastic form of a relation between things. The fetish character arises from the peculiar social character of the labour that produces commodities.

**Application and limit:** our analogy is structural, not an identity. Lean is not a commodity in the precise Marxian sense, and formal verification is not capitalist exchange-value. The proposed epistemic fetishism concerns the appearance that a collectively produced epistemic capacity inheres autonomously in the machine. Explain the analogy and its limits explicitly.

## 5. Lacan: the subject-supposed-to-know and the Big Other

### 5.1 *Seminar XI: The Four Fundamental Concepts of Psychoanalysis*

Jacques Lacan, *The Seminar, Book XI: The Four Fundamental Concepts of Psychoanalysis*, trans. Alan Sheridan (New York: W. W. Norton, 1977), especially Chapter XVIII, “Of the Subject Who Is Supposed to Know, of the First Dyad, and of the Good,” beginning at p. 230 in the Sheridan edition. Contents and session locator: https://www.lacan.com/seminars1a.htm

The subject-supposed-to-know is introduced in the context of transference and the analyst's position. Lacan's formulation is not a general synonym for trusting an expert, nor a claim that the analyst or machine literally possesses complete knowledge.

**Application:** this concept can illuminate how a knowing position is attributed to a formal system, expert, or institution. Use it only as a supporting concept and do not psychologize ordinary members of the public from media texts.

### 5.2 The Big Other and “there is no Other of the Other”

The formulation “there is no Other of the Other” belongs to Lacan's broader argument that no metalanguage or higher symbolic authority can provide an ultimate guarantee for the symbolic order. Relevant primary locations include *Seminar XVI: From an Other to the other* and *Seminar XX: Encore*; in Seminar XX, the barred Other is linked to the absence of a final guarantee. See the seminar text/contents and scholarly discussion: https://www.lacan.com/seminars1a.htm and https://www.lacan.com/symptom9/.

Do not cite Seminar XI alone as the source for the complete “no Other of the Other” thesis. In the paper, distinguish (i) the subject-supposed-to-know in Seminar XI, (ii) the Big Other as the symbolic locus, and (iii) the later thesis that there is no Other of the Other/no final metalanguage.

**Application:** Lean is not the Big Other. It may be positioned as a technical credential within an institutional-symbolic order that behaves as if a final guarantor were available. The Lacanian point is precisely that the guarantee cannot be made complete.

## 6. Close prior work: what the paper must not claim as new

### 6.1 Hardwig (1985)

John Hardwig, “Epistemic Dependence,” *The Journal of Philosophy* 82(7) (1985), 335–349. DOI: https://doi.org/10.2307/2026523.

Hardwig explains why rational knowers may depend on experts whose evidence and competence they cannot personally reproduce. Use him to establish the legitimacy and structure of epistemic dependence, not as the main explanation of the symbolic function of Lean.

### 6.2 De Millo, Lipton, and Perlis (1979)

Richard A. De Millo, Richard J. Lipton, and Alan J. Perlis, “Social Processes and Proofs of Theorems and Programs,” *Communications of the ACM* 22(5) (1979), 271–280. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/359104.359106.

The paper argues that mathematical confidence develops through social processes of criticism, communication, simplification, and acceptance, and contrasts this with formal program verification. Engage it as a foundational social account of proof acceptance. Do not simply repeat its argument or treat it as having anticipated Lean's modern kernel-based practice in every respect.

### 6.3 Kallel and El Louadi (2026)

Maher Kallel and Mohamed El Louadi, “Verification Abundance, Adjudication Scarcity: What Happens to Mathematical Knowledge When Proof Checking Becomes Free,” arXiv:2608.28997 (29 August 2026): https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.28997.

They distinguish L1 derivational validity, L2 representational fidelity, and L3 epistemic significance, and argue that automated checking transfers the burden to human adjudication. This is a close prior contribution. Our paper must not claim these distinctions or the scarcity of adjudication as its original discovery.

### 6.4 Public trust in science

Iordanou, Antoniou, and De Vos, “Public Trust in Science: A Systematic Literature Review,” *Journal of Academic Ethics* 24 (2026), article 56. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10805-026-09732-5.

The review synthesizes 124 studies and identifies source, message, receiver, integrity, transparency, inclusion, and communication factors. Use it as empirical background. The original contribution is not a new generic model of public trust.

## 7. Three-chapter theoretical architecture

### Chapter 3.1 — The Ideological Fantasy of Scientific Certainty

Argument sequence:
1. Scientific knowledge is socially consequential but not directly inspectable by most citizens.
2. Lean makes one dimension of mathematical warrant visible and repeatable.
3. Public discourse can turn this bounded procedure into an image of closure or finality.
4. Žižekian fantasy explains how this image can structure the social practice of relying on science, not merely misinform individuals.
5. Counterargument: the public may simply rely rationally on a limited credential; show what extra explanatory work fantasy performs.

### Chapter 3.2 — From Commodity Fetishism to Epistemic Fetishism

Argument sequence:
1. Reconstruct Marx's account of social relations appearing as relations between things.
2. Reconstruct Žižek's attention to the form through which social relations become effective and misrecognized.
3. Map the collective labour behind a Lean result: theorem choice, formalization, libraries, software, checking, semantic validation, expert interpretation, and institutional recognition.
4. Show how public representations may attribute the resulting authority to Lean as an autonomous property.
5. Define epistemic fetishism as the paper's own extension and specify the evidence threshold.
6. Distinguish fetishism from accurate shorthand and from justified confidence in the kernel.

### Chapter 3.3 — The Big Other and the Missing Guarantee

Argument sequence:
1. Define the Big Other as the symbolic locus of norms and authorization, not a super-knower.
2. Explain the subject-supposed-to-know in its specific transference context, then keep it analytically distinct.
3. Explain “there is no Other of the Other”: no final metalanguage or ultimate symbolic guarantor.
4. Show how science nevertheless requires practical procedures of authorization and public credentials.
5. Position Lean as one credential within this order, not as the Big Other itself.
6. Derive the paradox: the more visible the technical guarantee becomes, the easier it may be to mistake a local guarantee for a final one.

## 8. Conceptual safeguards

- “Ideological fantasy” is not mere optimism, wishful thinking, or a false belief.
- “Fetishism” is not mere admiration of technology; it concerns the form in which social relations appear.
- “Cynical ideology” is not established merely because a text contains both a caveat and a strong headline. It requires evidence of acknowledged limits and continued practical reproduction of an enlarged guarantee.
- The Big Other is not Lean, an institution, or a person. Lean may be positioned as a credential in a symbolic order.
- The subject-supposed-to-know is not a generic label for trust in experts or software.
- “Epistemic fetishism” is our proposed analytic extension, not a direct quotation or concept named by Žižek.
- The commodity analogy is structural and must not erase the historical specificity of Marx's critique of capitalist commodity production.
- A formally correct Lean proof is genuinely valuable. Ideology critique concerns the social meaning and possible enlargement of its authority, not the falsity of its technical result.

## 9. Acceptance criteria for the primary-text phase

1. The exact editions and page locators for Žižek, Marx, and Lacan are fixed.
2. The three concepts are reconstructed from primary texts without conflating fantasy, fetishism, Big Other, and subject-supposed-to-know.
3. The Sloterdijk origin/context of the cynical formula is accurately attributed.
4. The paper's proposed term “epistemic fetishism” is explicitly marked as an original extension.
5. The close prior contributions of Hardwig, De Millo et al., and Kallel & El Louadi are incorporated into a novelty-boundary table.
6. The three theory chapters form a connected argument and identify what empirical evidence would support or falsify each mechanism.
