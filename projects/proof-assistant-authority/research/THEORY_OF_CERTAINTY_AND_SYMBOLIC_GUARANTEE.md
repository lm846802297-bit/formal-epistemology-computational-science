# Lean, social trust, and the symbolic guarantee of certainty

**Project:** *In Lean We Trust? The Ideology of Machine-Checked Proofs in Science*  
**Date:** 27 November 2026  
**Status:** theoretical research memo; hypotheses and conceptual distinctions, not final conclusions.

## 1. Central problem

The paper does not ask merely why Lean is reliable or whether users should trust it. It asks why a society that cannot personally inspect most of its scientific knowledge may invest a technical verification system with symbolic significance as a guarantee of objectivity, rigor, or certainty.

**Guiding question:** Why does contemporary society place such value on technologies that transform complex knowledge claims into formally checkable objects, and how do these technologies reshape the social understanding of scientific truth?

The question must not presuppose that society necessarily needs absolute certainty, that all trust is ideological, or that Lean itself is a final guarantor. The empirical task is to identify the functions performed by public representations of verification.

## 2. Three questions and their analytical distinction

### 2.1 Where does epistemic authority reside?

Possible objects of trust include:
- the AI system that proposes or discovers a result;
- the Lean kernel and trusted computing base;
- the formalization team that selects definitions and encodes the theorem;
- domain experts who judge whether the formal statement means the intended mathematical claim;
- universities, companies, journals, and mathematical communities that assess significance;
- the broader scientific institution and its practices of correction, criticism, and replication.

These are not mutually exclusive. Public trust may be distributed across a chain, and an audience may trust the result without trusting every component equally.

### 2.2 How can collective epistemic labour become a machine property?

The proposed mechanism of epistemic fetishism is a representational displacement:

**collective work and institutional relations → apparently autonomous property of the machine.**

The relevant human and social work includes choosing the conjecture, fixing the formal statement, selecting definitions and libraries, translating informal mathematics, debugging proof terms, checking semantic correspondence, assessing novelty, and explaining the result to the public.

Fetishism is not established by the mere fact that a headline names Lean. It requires evidence that the public or institutional representation makes the machine appear to possess an authority that actually depends on these relations, while the relevant relations recede from view.

### 2.3 Why is a symbolic guarantee of certainty attractive?

A society organized around highly specialized knowledge faces a structural asymmetry: scientific claims affect everyone, while the means of assessing them are distributed unequally. A visible procedure of checking can make reliability publicly legible. It can compress a complex chain of testimony into an apparently inspectable credential.

Potential functions of the guarantee:
1. **Cognitive function:** offers a tractable proxy for assessing claims that lay audiences cannot reproduce.
2. **Institutional function:** supplies a visible sign of procedural rigor and accountability.
3. **Communicative function:** turns complex, conditional results into a concise public narrative.
4. **Affective function:** may reduce anxiety generated by uncertainty and dependence.
5. **Legitimating function:** may help institutions present a claim as settled or objective.

These are hypotheses. Evidence may show that Lean is used mainly as one limited credential rather than as a final guarantee. The analysis must allow this possibility.

## 3. The key theoretical insight: verification does not abolish uncertainty; it relocates it

A Lean kernel can reduce uncertainty about whether a proof term follows from a formal statement under a specified environment. It does not eliminate uncertainty about whether the formal statement captures the intended problem, whether the assumptions are appropriate, whether the result is novel or significant, or how it bears on the world.

The uncertainty is therefore relocated across levels:

1. **Object-level uncertainty:** is the mathematical or scientific claim true?
2. **Formal uncertainty:** does the proof term establish the encoded proposition?
3. **Representational uncertainty:** does the encoded proposition mean what the research community intended?
4. **Interpretive uncertainty:** what does the result establish, and what follows from it?
5. **Institutional uncertainty:** have appropriate experts and institutions assessed the claim?
6. **Public uncertainty:** what can non-specialists reasonably infer from the available testimony and communication?

Formal checking can sharply reduce uncertainty at level 2. Its success may increase the importance of levels 3–6. The technical guarantee does not remove dependence; it changes where dependence is located.

## 4. What Kallel and El Louadi (2026) already establish—and what remains open

Maher Kallel and Mohamed El Louadi, “Verification Abundance, Adjudication Scarcity: What Happens to Mathematical Knowledge When Proof Checking Becomes Free,” arXiv:2608.28997 (29 August 2026): https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.28997.

The paper distinguishes derivational validity (L1), representational fidelity (L2), and epistemic significance (L3). It argues that automated proof checking does not remove the human adjudication burden; it transfers it to the interpretation of formal statements and assessment of significance. It develops a taxonomy of representational mismatch and measures artefact sizes in an OpenAI Lean corpus.

**Overlap:** this paper already establishes that a kernel's proof check does not settle whether the formal statement captures the intended informal question, nor whether the result matters. We must not claim that this distinction, or the scarcity of expert adjudication, is our original contribution.

**Our proposed difference:** the present project asks how this bounded technical guarantee acquires social and symbolic authority beyond the mathematical community. It examines public trust, media representation, institutional legitimation, and the ideological functions of a visible certainty credential. Kallel and El Louadi focus on the epistemic economy of mathematical adjudication; this project focuses on the wider social life of the verification credential.

Their account should be treated as a close prior contribution and engaged directly in the literature review, not as an incidental citation.

## 5. Hardwig and Žižek: complementary but non-identical tasks

### 5.1 Hardwig: why reliance can be rational

Hardwig's “Epistemic Dependence” (1985) explains why a knower may rationally depend on experts whose evidence and competence they cannot personally reproduce. In modern science, the public's lack of direct access to experiments, data, and proofs is not automatically a defect. Division of epistemic labour is unavoidable.

Hardwig helps distinguish warranted trust from blind deference. The relevant questions concern competence, honesty, evidence, accountability, and the structure of expert testimony.

### 5.2 Žižek: what symbolic function does the guarantee perform?

Žižek adds a critical question that is not reducible to whether a particular act of trust is justified: how does a social order organize its relation to uncertainty and the desire for a guarantee?

- **Ideological fantasy:** a visible technical procedure may stage the image that scientific uncertainty can be mastered or closed.
- **Fetishism:** the reliability produced by collective labour and institutional relations may appear as a self-contained property of Lean or AI.
- **Cynical disavowal:** known limitations may coexist with practices that continue to invoke a stronger guarantee. This requires evidence of acknowledged limits and continued practical reproduction; it cannot be inferred from a caveat alone.
- **Big Other:** Lean is not the Big Other. The question is whether Lean is positioned as one credential within a symbolic order in which science, experts, institutions, and verification practices are expected to guarantee knowledge.

Hardwig asks when dependence is epistemically warranted. Žižek asks what desire, fantasy, and social function are organized through the form of the guarantee. The second question supplements rather than replaces the first.

## 6. A provisional mechanism model

**A. Social condition:** scientific knowledge is complex, specialized, and not directly surveyable by most citizens.

**B. Need for mediation:** citizens rely on experts, institutions, communication channels, and public indicators of quality.

**C. Technical credential:** Lean provides a highly specific and reproducible check of a formal proof against a formal proposition.

**D. Symbolic translation:** institutions and media may present this check as a visible sign of rigor, objectivity, or finality.

**E. Public uptake:** audiences may use the credential as a proxy for trust in the result, the experts, the institution, or science generally—or may reject it because AI involvement reduces perceived human accountability.

**F. Ideological possibility:** when the credential is made to stand for more than it checks, the technical guarantee can support an ideological fantasy or fetishistic representation.

This is not a deterministic causal sequence. Each transition must be evidenced; public trust may increase, decrease, or remain unchanged.

## 7. Four Lean cases as tests of the mechanism

| Case | What Lean contributes | Main question about symbolic authority |
|---|---|---|
| Riemann | extensive checking of formalized RH/GRH statements | Can machine certification become a public proxy for mathematical understanding and acceptance? |
| Navier–Stokes | kernel-checked formal declarations | Does public authority attach to Lean even when correspondence to the separate written proof is disputed? |
| Erdős | proof search and formal checking embedded in a research workflow | Does explicit human statement validation make the credential more transparent and less fetishized? |
| Liquid Tensor | formalization of a specified theorem in visible collaboration | Does visible collective labour resist the appearance of autonomous machine authority? |

The Waring/GPT case remains a contrast only, because no Lean certificate is documented at the cut-off.

## 8. Evidence needed for each claim

- **Authority transfer:** compare which actors are named as responsible and which are described as guarantors in institutional and media texts; use public surveys only for actual audience evaluations.
- **Fetishism:** show the underlying collective process, identify the public representation, and document what is foregrounded or displaced. Do not infer from a catchy headline alone.
- **Fantasy of certainty:** identify recurring language of proof, verification, solution, certainty, or finality; compare it with the exact scope and explicit qualifications in primary sources.
- **Cynical disavowal:** require evidence that a relevant institution or actor acknowledges a limit and nevertheless continues a practice that depends on the stronger claim. No such conclusion is currently established.

## 9. Propositions for the paper

**P1 — Proxy proposition:** In conditions of limited public access to specialized evidence, machine verification can function as a proxy credential for the reliability of a scientific claim.

**P2 — Mediation proposition:** The social authority of that credential depends on the perceived competence and accountability of the human and institutional chain that connects the formal proposition to the intended scientific claim.

**P3 — Visibility proposition:** Public visibility of human contribution and semantic validation can change whether Lean is interpreted as a bounded checking tool or as an autonomous guarantor.

**P4 — Relocation proposition:** Increasing the reliability and scalability of formal checking reduces one class of uncertainty while relocating attention and responsibility to semantic, interpretive, and institutional levels.

**P5 — Fetishism proposition:** Epistemic fetishism becomes plausible when public discourse treats the machine's technical property as self-sufficient while obscuring the collective relations that make its result relevant.

These propositions are provisional and must be compared against legitimate trust, institutional reputation, ordinary shorthand, and public scepticism.

## 10. Provisional conclusion

The most promising philosophical claim is not that society blindly trusts Lean, nor that technical verification is a false guarantee. It is that **a society unable to inspect most of its scientific knowledge may use machine verification as a visible symbol of reliability; the symbol can be epistemically warranted within a defined scope and ideologically enlarged beyond that scope.**

The originality of the paper must lie in explaining the social and ideological function of this symbolic guarantee, not in rediscovering the distinction between formal validity and semantic correspondence.
