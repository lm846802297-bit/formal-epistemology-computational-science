# Literature and public-case pilot: literature map and first findings

**Research project:** *In Lean We Trust? The Ideology of Machine-Checked Proofs in Science*  
**Research phase:** Initial literature and public-text pilot  
**Status:** In progress; not a completed systematic review  
**Scope:** Public textual construction of epistemic and ideological authority. No inference about actual reader belief or author motive.

## 1. Main finding

The initial survey supports a viable research question, but not yet a conclusion that proof-assistant discourse systematically overstates what has been checked. Existing social epistemology already explains why non-experts rationally depend on experts and institutions. Science-communication studies show that simplification and popularization are themselves practices through which expertise and authority are organized. Recent work on artificial epistemic authorities directly discusses whether AI systems can count as epistemic authorities.

The distinctive proposed contribution must therefore be narrower: compare the exact scope of formal verification with the scope attributed to it in project-authored and independent public texts, and identify when the latter attributes a wider scientific or mathematical verdict than the formal artifact and its documented semantic bridge warrant. Žižek's ideology critique may explain how a genuine local reliability can become a support for broader fantasies of scientific certainty; this remains a hypothesis to be tested against the cases, not a predetermined verdict.

## 2. Literature map

### 2.1 Epistemic dependence and expert testimony

- John Hardwig, “Epistemic Dependence,” *The Journal of Philosophy* 82(7) (1985), 335–349. JSTOR stable record: https://www.jstor.org/stable/2026523. Foundational account of rational dependence on experts when an individual cannot personally possess or assess all relevant evidence.
- Ben Almassi, “Experts, Evidence, and Epistemic Independence,” *Spontaneous Generations* 1(1) (2007). DOI: https://doi.org/10.4245/sponge.v1i1.2972. Discusses Hardwig's account and the tension between rational dependence and epistemic independence.
- Fernando Broncano-Berrocal and Jesús Vega-Encabo, “A Taxonomy of Types of Epistemic Dependence,” *Synthese* 197 (2020), 2745–2763. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-019-02233-6. Offers a goal-relative taxonomy of epistemic dependence.
- Jeroen de Ridder, “How to Trust a Scientist,” *Studies in History and Philosophy of Science Part A* (2022). DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.shpsa.2022.02.003. Useful for the content and justification of trust among scientists.
- George Kwasi Barimah, “Second-Order Assessment of Scientific Expert Claims and Sharing Epistemic Burdens in Science Communication,” *Episteme* (2022). DOI: https://doi.org/10.1017/epi.2022.11. Explains why non-experts cannot easily assess experts and why epistemic burdens should be shared in communication.

**Use in this paper:** Hardwig explains why delegation to a proof assistant, formalization expert, or research team can be rational. It does not by itself explain scope expansion. Dependence can be legitimate even when the dependent reader cannot inspect the proof. The key question becomes whether the chain of testimony preserves the distinction between what the kernel checked and what the scientific claim requires.

### 2.2 Science communication, popularization, and authority

- Stephen Hilgartner, “The Dominant View of Popularization: Conceptual Problems, Political Uses,” *Social Studies of Science* 20(3) (1990), 519–539. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1177/030631290020003006. Critiques the two-stage model of scientific knowledge followed by simplified public dissemination; shows how popularization discourse can sustain hierarchies of expertise.
- Harry Collins and Robert Evans, “The Third Wave of Science Studies: Studies of Expertise and Experience,” *Social Studies of Science* 32(2) (2002), 235–296. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1177/0306312702032002003. Distinguishes forms of expertise and the problem of extending technical decision authority.
- George Kwasi Barimah (2022), above. Relevant to second-order assessment and the allocation of epistemic burdens.
- Carrie Figdor, “Science Journalism and Epistemic Virtues in Science Communication: A Defense of Sincerity, Transparency, and Honesty,” *Episteme* (2023). DOI: https://doi.org/10.1017/epi.2023.38. Addresses epistemic virtues in science journalism and the relationship between institutional and individual communication.

**Use in this paper:** Do not treat every simplification as distortion. Code whether the scope is preserved, reasonably compressed, ambiguous, or substantively expanded. Analyze the public text as a communicative act, not as transparent access to the speaker's mental state.

### 2.3 Artificial and algorithmic epistemic authority

- “Artificial Epistemic Authorities,” *Social Epistemology* (2025). DOI: https://doi.org/10.1080/02691728.2025.2449602. Directly discusses whether AI systems can be recognized as artificial epistemic authorities, including identification and deference problems.

**Novelty implication:** It is no longer defensible to claim that this project is the first to ask whether non-human computational systems can acquire epistemic authority. The more specific contribution is the relation between a proof assistant's bounded verification relation and the broader scope of claims attributed to that verification in scientific communication. Proof assistants differ from general-purpose AI recommenders: their formal checking is tied to explicit propositions, definitions, axioms, and proof terms. The communication problem concerns how those bounded results are represented and connected to a target claim.

### 2.4 Žižek's critique of ideology and Lacanian background

- Slavoj Žižek, *The Sublime Object of Ideology* (1989). Principal primary source for the Žižekian account of ideology, including fantasy and the critique of cynical reason.
- Matthew Flisfeder, “There is no metalanguage; or, Truth has the structure of a fiction: The Žižekian system, between post-ideology and post-truth,” *Enrahonar* 70 (2023), 171–185. DOI: https://doi.org/10.5565/rev/enrahonar.1420. A useful secondary map of the Big Other, cynical ideology, commodity fetishism, and Žižek's ideology critique.
- Daniel Hourigan, *The Phantasmatic Subject of Technology: Slavoj Žižek, Techne, and the Abyss* (2009), Griffith University doctoral thesis. https://doi.org/10.25904/1912/3124. A prior Žižekian critique of technology and the fetishization of technology; it prevents any claim that this project is the first to apply Žižek to technology.
- Jack Black, “Examining the mechanism of disavowal and its two forms: Cynical disavowal and fetishistic disavowal,” *Theory & Psychology* 35(1) (2025), 117–135. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1177/09593543241308199. Useful for distinguishing cynical and fetishistic disavowal.
- Jacques Lacan, *The Seminar, Book XI: The Four Fundamental Concepts of Psychoanalysis* (1964), especially the sessions on transference and “the subject who is supposed to know.”
- Jacques Lacan, “Proposition of 9 October 1967 on the Psychoanalyst of the School.”
- Jacques Lacan, *The Seminar, Book XVII: The Other Side of Psychoanalysis* (1969–70), for the discourse of the university and the relation between knowledge and authority.
- Slavoj Žižek, “The Interpassive Subject,” https://www.lacan.com/zizek-pompidou.htm.

**Conceptual use:** Žižek's ideological fantasy can help analyze how local formal reliability may support an image of scientific certainty; fetishism can help analyze how collective epistemic labour appears as an autonomous property of the machine; cynical ideology can help analyze cases where known limitations coexist with continued authority-laden practices. These are distinct mechanisms and must not be collapsed into a single label.

**Conceptual caution:** The subject supposed to know is not simply a person or device that knows more, nor a synonym for trust in technology. It concerns a position attributed within the structure of transference. Interpassivity is related but not interchangeable. Public text analysis can establish representations and attributions of authority; it cannot by itself establish unconscious motives, transference, or actual reader belief.

## 3. Initial formalization cases

### 3.1 Lean: quantum cryptography and BB84 (2026)

Maor Ben-Shahar, Jonathan Morag, Dirk Englund, and Kfir Sulimany, “Quantum Cryptography and Autoformalization in Lean,” arXiv:2610.17286 (2026). https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17286

Primary source inspected: full text. Key materials:
- Abstract and introduction claim an end-to-end machine-verified composable finite-key security proof for entanglement-based qubit BB84 against coherent attacks.
- Theorem 1 states a finite-key composable security bound in diamond norm.
- Theorem 2 bounds abort probability for an honest memoryless channel.
- The protocol is encoded in a Lean language whose constructors enforce local operations and classical communication (LOCC).
- The paper explicitly distinguishes Lean kernel checking from human review of whether definitions and theorem statements match the intended physical protocol and security claim.
- It states that the model is finite-dimensional and describes further extensions required for prepare-and-measure protocols and optical QKD with decoy states.

**Provisional code:** scope-aware author presentation / valuable negative case. The authors explicitly separate kernel verification from semantic audit. The paper's broad phrase “end-to-end” is then narrowed by a detailed protocol definition, assumptions, and theorem statements. Do not code this as overclaim without a specific public statement that omits or contradicts these qualifications.

### 3.2 Isabelle/HOL: Schutz axioms for Minkowski spacetime (2022)

Richard Schmoetten, Jake E. Palmer, and Jacques D. Fleuriot, “Towards Formalising Schutz’ Axioms for Minkowski Spacetime in Isabelle/HOL,” *Journal of Automated Reasoning* 66 (2022), 953–988. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10817-022-09643-1

Primary article inspected in full. It mechanizes Schutz's axiomatic system and theorems about temporal order. The authors report that some results needed additional steps, alternative approaches, or corrections to Schutz's prose. Crucially, they state that they have not formalized a model of the theory, while expressing confidence in the formulation of their axioms of order and incidence.

**Provisional code:** scope-aware author presentation / negative case. The distinction between axiomatization, theorem verification, and model construction is explicit. The formalization is not presented as an empirical test of special relativity.

### 3.3 Coq: Robertson-type uncertainty relation (2015)

Takaaki Masuhara, Toru Kuriyama, Masakazu Yoshida, and Jun Cheng, “Formal Verification of Robertson-Type Uncertainty Relation,” *Journal of Quantum Information Science* 5 (2015), 58–70. DOI: https://doi.org/10.4236/jqis.2015.52008

Primary paper inspected. Its abstract says the relation is verified in Coq and frames the work as being “toward verifying unconditional security” of QKD. That wording may be a statement of motivation rather than a claim that the full protocol's security is established. The theorem statement, source code, conclusion, and any external retelling must be inspected before coding.

**Provisional code:** ambiguous / requires context. Do not infer substantive scope expansion from the abstract alone.

### 3.4 HOL Light + Isabelle: Flyspeck / Kepler conjecture (2017)

Thomas Hales et al., “A Formal Proof of the Kepler Conjecture,” *Forum of Mathematics, Pi* (2017). DOI: https://doi.org/10.1017/fmp.2017.1

The published paper is the official account of the completed Flyspeck project and describes a formal proof of the Kepler conjecture using HOL Light and Isabelle. The project formalized both traditional text-based proof components and computational parts of the proof.

**Independent retelling located:** Bob Yirka, “Team announces construction of a formal computer-verified proof of the Kepler conjecture,” *Phys.org*, 13 August 2014: https://phys.org/news/2014-08-team-formal-computer-verified-proof-kepler.html. The report says the original proof was converted to code, HOL Light and Isabelle checked it, and the proof of the conjecture was verified. The wording “puts to rest any doubts” is rhetorically strong, but the primary project and published account describe a formalization covering both the text proof and computational calculations. This is a useful **scope-preserving negative case**: a strong public conclusion can be warranted when the formal artifact covers the full target theorem and its proof components.

### 3.5 Lean: Liquid Tensor Experiment (2020–2022)

Peter Scholze, “Liquid Tensor Experiment,” *Experimental Mathematics* 31(2) (2022), 349–354. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1080/10586458.2021.1926016.

Primary public sources:
- Formal blueprint: https://leanprover-community.github.io/liquid/sect0001.html
- Completion announcement: https://leanprover-community.github.io/blog/posts/lte-final/
- Scholze's contemporaneous account: https://xenaproject.wordpress.com/2021/06/05/half-a-year-of-the-liquid-tensor-experiment-amazing-developments/
- Follow-up explanation: https://xenaproject.wordpress.com/2022/09/12/beyond-the-liquid-tensor-experiment/

The challenge concerned a precise theorem about Ext groups of condensed real vector spaces, not the wholesale verification of condensed mathematics. The Lean blueprint gives the theorem statement and links to the formal code. The Lean community's completion post says the main theorem of liquid vector spaces was formally verified on 14 July 2022.

**Independent retelling:** Kevin Hartnett, “Proof Assistant Makes Jump to Big-League Math,” *Quanta Magazine*, 28 July 2021: https://www.quantamagazine.org/lean-computer-program-confirms-peter-scholze-proof-20210728/. The article's lead says mathematicians using Lean “have verified the accuracy of a difficult theorem”; its body says the formalization covered the part Scholze was most unsure about. Scholze's 5 June 2021 account says the challenge was not complete, Theorem 9.4 had been formalized, and human checking was still needed to ensure the formal statement matched the intended mathematics. In a 15 June comment, he says the human interaction and scrutiny, not the computer as such, gave him confidence. This is coded **ambiguous / hybrid authority attribution**, not confirmed scope expansion.

### 3.6 Lean: Ax-Prover (2025/2026)

“Ax-Prover: A Deep Reasoning Agentic Framework for Theorem Proving in Mathematics and Quantum Physics,” *Machine Learning: Science and Technology* 7 (2026), DOI: https://doi.org/10.1088/2632-2153/ae9452; preprint: https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.12787.

This is a current case in which an LLM-based agentic workflow uses Lean to formalize and check results in mathematics and quantum cryptography. It reports human-in-the-loop work in cryptography and quantum information.

**Provisional code:** candidate for studying the attribution of epistemic authority across LLM, domain expert, and Lean kernel. It is not a clean “machine alone” case. The distinctions between generation, proof checking, and domain-expert guidance must be preserved. Independent public retellings remain to be collected.

### 3.7 Lean: Riemann hypothesis and Dirichlet L-functions, Alpöge–Claude (November 2026)

Levent Alpöge announced on 20 November 2026 that he had found a proof of the Riemann hypothesis working with Claude, with a Lean 4 archive. The original archive contains 175,187 files and approximately 17.4 million lines. The five headline theorems state two formulations of RH and three results for Dirichlet L-functions.

Primary sources:
- Alpöge's announcement and note: https://alpo.ge/
- Joint Lean FRO / Mathlib Initiative statement (21 November): https://leanprover-community.github.io/blog/posts/riemann-hypothesis/
- Andrew Sutherland's MIT verification record: https://math.mit.edu/~asutherland/ (RH proof verification files)
- Michael Rubinstein's Waterloo verification record: https://www.math.uwaterloo.ca/~mrubinst/alpoge_claude_grh/
- Current dated verification summary: https://riemannhypothesissolved.com/status/
- Nature, “Mathematicians astounded by AI and human-written proofs of iconic Riemann hypothesis” (20 November; updated 23 November): https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03812-7
- AP, “Anthropic worker claims to have solved a Holy Grail of math problems with the help of AI”: https://apnews.com/article/4fe8b69a891f160f9e7f0c85af8c55c8

**Verification status as of 27 November 2026:** the Lean FRO and Mathlib Initiative jointly certified the formal statement and proof of RH on 21 November. Independent checking reports include full compilation and kernel replay of all 175,187 modules, independent proof checkers, theorem-statement comparisons, and use of only Lean's three standard axioms (`propext`, `Classical.choice`, `Quot.sound`). On 26 November, MIT and Waterloo reports record successful paranoid Comparator checks of Luke Sernau's shortened rewrite; the Waterloo report records a successful compact-edition run. These are substantial and specific formal verification results.

**What remains distinct:** a readable mathematical reconstruction and broad human understanding of why the argument works are still developing. Rubinstein's 265-page working guide (22 November, version 2) explicitly reduces the RH proof to a central inequality and thirteen hypotheses, and says the central inequality is not yet proved in that guide. The formal proof is therefore strongly machine-checked, while mathematical exposition, independent expert digestion, conventional peer review, and Clay Prize recognition remain separate milestones. The Clay Prize rules require publication in a qualifying outlet, a two-year period, and general acceptance.

**Provisional code:** high-confidence formal proof checking; human mathematical understanding and publication status remain distinct. This is not a simple example of overclaim. It is the strongest contemporary case for analyzing how formal certification, expert testimony, readable explanation, and public declarations of a solved Millennium problem interact.

### 3.8 GPT-6.2 Astra: Waring's problem (November 2026)

Sam Chow and Rainer Dietmann, “A linear bound in Waring's problem,” arXiv:2611.29821, submitted 22 November 2026: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29821.

The preprint claims (G(k) \asymp k), equivalently a linear upper bound (G(k) \ll k) for the least number of (k)-th powers needed to represent every sufficiently large integer. Its AI-use statement says the work was carried out by GPT-6.2 Astra, rewritten by the authors, and “refereed” by GPT-6.2 Astra. The claim is not peer reviewed or independently checked; no Lean formalization or independent proof certificate is documented in the sources inspected. The paper also mentions a stronger numerical constant (8.4k) without proving it in the preprint.

**Provisional code:** AI-generated mathematical claim with human authorship/revision, but without independent verification. This case is analytically distinct from Alpöge–Claude: it concerns the authority of a model-generated conventional mathematical argument and the unusual claim of AI self-refereeing, rather than a Lean-certified proof. It should not be presented as a GPT theorem already established.

### 3.9 OpenAI internal system: Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness (September 2026)

OpenAI, “On the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize Problem,” 8 September 2026: https://openai.com/index/navier-stokes-solution/

OpenAI reports that an internal multi-agent system found an analytical proof of finite-time singularity for a three-dimensional incompressible Navier–Stokes flow with a smooth external force, and that it released both a written proof and a Lean formalization. The company says the internal model used was significantly more capable than GPT-6 Astra; it should therefore not be labelled simply as a GPT-generated proof. The public write-up describes roughly 10,000 concurrent agents and subsequent Lean formalization/verification.

**Provisional code:** institutionally presented AI-assisted proof claim with a written argument and formalization. The scope of the exact Clay problem variant, formal statement, Lean build, and independent mathematical review must be audited separately. OpenAI explicitly says it does not intend to claim the Millennium Prize for the result. This is a useful comparison with the Riemann case because the AI contribution, proof exposition, and formal verification are presented through a corporate research announcement.

### 3.10 Google DeepMind: AI-driven formal proof search on open problems (2026)

George Tsoukalas et al., “Advancing Mathematics Research with AI-Driven Formal Proof Search,” *Science* 394 (6820) (8 October 2026), 234–239. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aej2213; preprint: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.22763.

The AlphaProof Nexus system reports solving 9 of 353 formalized open Erdős problems and proving 44 of 492 OEIS conjectures, with the Lean proofs released. The paper describes an agentic workflow alternating LLM-generated proof attempts with Lean compiler feedback. It also reports human mathematical checking of whether formal statements correspond to intended problems.

**Provisional code:** published, formal-proof-search case and a useful contrast to individual headline-grabbing Millennium claims. The denominator matters: the 353 Erdős problems were those already formalized in the Formal Conjectures repository, not all open Erdős problems. The case permits analysis of how a carefully quantified research result is communicated without implying that AI has solved mathematics generally.

## 4. First public-text comparison

Two useful comparisons have emerged:

1. **Liquid Tensor Experiment:** a hybrid-authority case. The public headline and article foreground Lean's confirmation, while Scholze's contemporaneous statements emphasize a specific formalized sub-result, the incompleteness of the wider challenge at that date, and the role of human mathematical scrutiny. This case is ambiguous rather than a proven overclaim.
2. **Flyspeck:** a scope-preserving negative control. The public claim that the Kepler conjecture was formally verified is supported by the completed formalization of the full mathematical proof and computational components.

The contrast prevents the project from assuming that strong public language is inherently ideological or misleading.

## 5. Coding scheme and stopping rule

Use four primary codes:
- **Accurate:** explicit claim supported by the formal artifact and does not imply a broader target.
- **Reasonable compression:** details omitted but intended scope preserved by context or qualification.
- **Ambiguous:** materially different readings remain possible or relevant evidence is missing.
- **Substantive scope expansion:** claim exceeds what the formal artifact and linked semantic bridge establish; specify the missing inferential link.

For every record, code the target level separately:
1. local formal deduction;
2. record translation/interoperability;
3. correspondence to source mathematics/physics;
4. model/protocol instantiation;
5. empirical/physical target;
6. public verdict that the scientific question is settled.

Stop or narrow the thesis if the pilot contains only accurate shorthand or cannot reliably distinguish expansion from ambiguity. Do not infer belief, motive, or Lacanian transference from public text alone.

## 8. Society-wide research scope and four-case update (27 November 2026)

The research object is now explicitly society-wide. The project asks how machine verification becomes a publicly legible symbol of the reliability of scientific knowledge. The chain under study runs from result production through formal checking, expert and institutional appraisal, public communication, and audience interpretation. The new module [PUBLIC_TRUST_AND_RECEPTION.md](PUBLIC_TRUST_AND_RECEPTION.md) documents the survey baseline and the first public-reception sample.

### 8.1 Riemann hypothesis — Claude + Lean

The Lean FRO / Mathlib Initiative statement (21 November 2026) reports that the approximately 175,187-module, 17.4-million-line Lean archive compiles, that its formal theorem corresponds to the vetted Mathlib statement of RH, and that exported proof terms pass multiple independent checkers:
- Alpöge's announcement: https://alpo.ge/

- MIT verification materials: https://math.mit.edu/~asutherland/

The formal-check status is substantially stronger than an unreviewed natural-language AI proof. The same-day human-written Masullo draft is a comparison case in public authorship and reception, not an accepted proof.

### 8.2 Waring's problem — GPT-6.2 Astra

Sam Chow and Rainer Dietmann, “A linear bound in Waring's problem,” arXiv:2611.29821 (22 November 2026): https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.29821

Theorem 1.1 claims (G(k) \ll k); with the lower bound (G(k)\geq k+1), this gives (G(k)\asymp k). The AI statement says the work was carried out by GPT-6.2 Astra, rewritten by the authors, and refereed by GPT-6.2 Astra. The preprint also says (G(k)\leq 8.4k) follows from more sophisticated reasoning but does not demonstrate that bound in the current version.

As of 27 November, this is an arXiv preprint without a Lean formalization, documented independent expert verification, or peer-reviewed publication. AI self-refereeing is not independent review. This case is analytically distinct from Lean-certified work: it foregrounds AI authorship and self-assessment without an external proof certificate.

### 8.3 Navier–Stokes — OpenAI multi-agent discovery + Lean

Primary sources:
- OpenAI announcement and paper: https://openai.com/index/navier-stokes-solution/
- Written proof: https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/32d9f210-8b73-45e0-91bc-82a30aef8a9a/navier-stokes.pdf
- Lean repository: https://github.com/openai/NavierStokesAndEuler
- Pinned repository commit: https://github.com/openai/NavierStokesAndEuler/commit/f9e8bc5b38b6e212696e8a30e3e91517af887bbd
- Formalization metadata: https://github.com/openai/NavierStokesAndEuler/blob/f9e8bc5b38b6e212696e8a30e3e91517af887bbd/formalization.yaml
- Independent critique: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08144
- OpenAI response reported by New Scientist: https://www.newscientist.com/article/2592824-openai-mistranslated-mathematics-into-code-for-its-navier-stokes-proof/

OpenAI's paper states a finite-time blow-up theorem for three-dimensional incompressible Navier–Stokes with smooth forcing and claims Clay alternatives (C) and (D). The repository records full formalization of the main results, no admitted placeholders, and only the standard Lean axioms; its review field says “self-assessed.” OpenAI says the discovery used an internal multi-agent system, while Lean formalization and verification took a further 17 hours via GPT-6 Astra. The internal discovery model and formalization model are not the same and must not be conflated.

Bastounis, Circelli, and Hansen compare the paper with the Lean source at commit f9e8bc5. They report (i) an (m+4) versus (m+5) derivative discrepancy in the inverse estimate of Lemma 8.6, and (ii) a difference in the pressure-flux estimate and proof route. They explicitly do not claim that the natural-language proof is correct or incorrect; their claim is that the Lean code is not a semantically faithful translation of the written proof. OpenAI told New Scientist it was aware of the mismatch and said it did not show either proof invalid, adding that it would rectify errors in the written proof as they are found.

**Interpretive boundary:** this is evidence of a mismatch between proof artifacts, not a refutation of the underlying Navier–Stokes theorem and not a kernel failure. It is a central case for distinguishing formal validity from semantic correspondence.

### 8.4 Erdős problems — Google DeepMind AlphaProof Nexus

George Tsoukalas et al., “Advancing mathematics research with AI-driven formal proof search,” *Science* 394 (6820) (8 October 2026), 234–239. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aej2213. PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42848891/

The paper reports 9 solutions among 353 open Erdős problems represented as Lean formal statements in the Formal Conjectures repository at the time, and 44 proofs among 492 attempted OEIS conjectures. The authors state that human experts validated the correspondence between the nine Lean statements and the original conjectures; the OEIS conjectures were manually reviewed as correctly formalized and previously unproven. The discussion explicitly says AI-generated informal proofs require careful expert validation and that formal verification can filter which proofs merit human review.

This is a useful scope-preserving case: the authors report denominators, formal statement selection, Lean verification, and human review rather than implying that AI has solved mathematics generally. The public-facing AAAS release is at https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1146436.


The Science Media Centre Spain collected contrasting expert reactions on 8 October 2026. Josep Curto argued that the simple generate-and-verify agent solved the same nine Erdős problems as the more elaborate system, questioned the cost accounting, and emphasized that Lean checks the written formal statement rather than its fidelity to the intended mathematical problem. Javier Aramayona raised the concern that a growing body of certified but not fully understood results could weaken mathematical understanding and researcher training. Other respondents emphasized the genuine research potential. Source: https://sciencemediacentre.es/en/alphaproof-nexus-new-ai-tool-designed-tackle-mathematical-proofs.

A separate audit by Carlos Toledo reports that the published Lean proofs replay under two kernels, but that three of the 38 published OEIS conjectures already had proofs before the preprint, two formal statements are degenerate relative to the intended conjectures, and the nine Erdős statements correspond to seven problem numbers with parts and variants. The auditor explicitly does not settle whether all Formal Conjectures statements match the original Erdős problems; its readings of the OEIS records are AI-generated and not specialist-confirmed. It therefore supplies a valuable secondary audit, not a final mathematical verdict. Source: https://carlostoledo.co/reports/alphaproof-nexus.html.

- “Trust in scientists and their role in society across 68 countries,” *Nature Human Behaviour* (2025), N=71,922. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-02090-5.
- Bretter et al., “Public skepticism about the use of AI in scientific research,” OSF preprint (5 March 2026), UK representative survey N=1,465. DOI: https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/3h5n6_v1.

The third source is directly about public attitudes to AI in research but not specifically machine-checked proofs. The Wellcome and Nature Human Behaviour surveys measure general trust in science/scientists, not attitudes to AI-assisted proofs. None can be used to claim that the four announcements caused a change in public trust.

### 8.6 Consequence for the theory

The cases do not support a single, undifferentiated claim that the public “believes the machine.” They identify at least four possible objects of trust:
1. the AI system's capacity to discover;
2. the proof assistant's capacity to check a formal proposition;
3. experts and institutions' capacity to validate semantic correspondence and significance;
4. the media account's capacity to represent what has actually been established.

A Žižekian interpretation must be earned by the evidence. Fetishism is a plausible reading when collective labour, formal statement selection, semantic translation, and expert review recede behind an apparently autonomous machine authority. Transparent accounts of human review and scope may instead resist that presentation. The current public-text evidence does not establish unconscious motives or population-wide belief.


### 8.6A Primary Žižek text: ideological fantasy as a structure of practice

The key passage for this project is in Slavoj Žižek, *The Sublime Object of Ideology* (Verso, 1989), especially pp. 28–30, where he revises the Marxian formula “they do not know it, but they are doing it.” The argument is not simply that people hold false beliefs. Ideological fantasy can structure social activity even where participants consciously know relevant facts or limitations. The illusion may operate in the practice and its social organization, rather than as a mistaken proposition in an individual's head.

- Bibliographic/publisher record: https://www.versobooks.com/products/1284-the-sublime-object-of-ideology
- Textual access for checking page references: https://www.thetedkarchive.com/library/slavoj-zizek-the-sublime-object-of-ideology
- Žižek's introduction, “The Spectre of Ideology,” in *Mapping Ideology* (Verso, 1994), distinguishes explicit doctrine, material institutional practices, and the implicit presuppositions reproduced within apparently non-ideological everyday practices: https://perpus.fauzy.eu.org/Politik/Slavoj%20Zizek%20%281994%29%20Mapping_Ideology.pdf
- Secondary conceptual map: Matthew Flisfeder (2023), https://doi.org/10.5565/rev/enrahonar.1420.

**Application to the present study:** A member of the public may know that they cannot personally inspect a Lean proof, and may even know that a formal proof establishes only a proposition under stated definitions and axioms. Yet their practical reliance on a public chain of certification may still treat machine verification as a guarantee of scientific certainty. This is not automatically irrational or ideological: reliance on experts and institutions can be justified. A Žižekian interpretation becomes plausible only if the practice or public representation turns a bounded technical check into an apparently self-sufficient guarantee while concealing the collective and semantic conditions that make the check relevant.

**Important restraint:** Do not label ordinary public trust as “cynical ideology.” Cynical ideology requires evidence that known limitations coexist with a practice that continues to reproduce the relevant illusion. The current surveys show public ambivalence about AI in science, not the specific disavowal mechanism Žižek describes. The Riemann and Navier–Stokes public discussions contain some explicit awareness of the gap between machine checking and human understanding; whether this awareness is nevertheless displaced in practical trust is an open question.

## 9. New theoretical literature and novelty boundary (27 November 2026)

### 9.1 Kallel & El Louadi: verification abundance and adjudication scarcity

Maher Kallel and Mohamed El Louadi, “Verification Abundance, Adjudication Scarcity: What Happens to Mathematical Knowledge When Proof Checking Becomes Free,” arXiv:2608.28997v1 (29 August 2026): https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.28997.

This is a close prior contribution and must be engaged directly. It distinguishes L1 derivational validity (kernel-checkable), L2 representational fidelity (whether the formal statement means the intended informal question), and L3 epistemic significance (novelty and disciplinary importance). It argues that cheaper machine checking transfers the verification burden to scarce expert adjudication. It analyzes a Lean proof corpus and develops a taxonomy of representational mismatch.

**Overlap:** the distinction between a valid Lean proof and the intended mathematical claim, and the scarcity of human adjudication, are already articulated. They cannot be presented as this paper's original contribution.

**Difference:** the present paper studies the social life of the verification credential beyond the mathematical community: public trust, media representation, institutional legitimation, and the symbolic/ideological function of a visible guarantee. Kallel and El Louadi focus on the epistemic economy of mathematical adjudication; *In Lean We Trust?* asks why that technical credential becomes socially meaningful and how it may shape public conceptions of scientific truth.

### 9.2 De Millo, Lipton & Perlis: the social process of proof

Richard A. De Millo, Richard J. Lipton, and Alan J. Perlis, “Social Processes and Proofs of Theorems and Programs,” *Communications of the ACM* 22(5) (1979), 271–280. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/359104.359106.

The paper argues that mathematical confidence is not produced by formal derivation alone; proofs become credible through social practices of reading, criticism, simplification, teaching, generalization, and acceptance. It is a historical precursor for the social dimension of proof. It should be read critically alongside later proof-assistant practice rather than treated as a final refutation of formalization.

### 9.3 Fetzer: formal verification and the represented object

James H. Fetzer, “Program Verification: The Very Idea,” *Communications of the ACM* 31(9) (1988), 1048–1063. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/48529.48530.

Fetzer distinguishes algorithms as logical structures from programs as causal models of those structures. His argument is about the gap between formal relations and the object the formal system is intended to represent. This is relevant background for semantic correspondence, but the paper must not simply transfer his conclusions about program behaviour to mathematical proof assistants without argument.

### 9.4 Public trust in science: systematic review

Kalypso Iordanou, Antonis Antoniou, and Maura De Vos, “Public Trust in Science: A Systematic Literature Review,” *Journal of Academic Ethics* 24 (2026), article 56. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10805-026-09732-5. Published 11 March 2026.

The review synthesizes 124 empirical studies and organizes trust factors into receiver, message, and source categories. It highlights research integrity, scientists' perceived integrity, transparency, epistemic inclusion, prior beliefs, values, communication channels, and open-science practices. It supports a society-wide and multi-factor account of trust, while cautioning against assuming that a single technical credential explains public confidence.

### 9.5 Consequence for the originality claim

The paper's originality must be located in the connection among (i) Lean as a visible technical credential, (ii) public and institutional mediation of scientific knowledge, and (iii) Žižek's account of ideological fantasy and fetishism. It should not claim to have discovered that formal proof checking does not establish semantic correspondence, that mathematical proof has a social dimension, or that public trust depends on source/message/receiver factors.
